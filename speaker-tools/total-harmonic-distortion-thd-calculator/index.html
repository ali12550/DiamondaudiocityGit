What Is Total Harmonic Distortion?

Definition: Total harmonic distortion (THD) is the ratio of all unwanted harmonic frequencies a device adds to the original (fundamental) signal, expressed as a percentage. When a 1 kHz tone passes through an amplifier and emerges with traces of 2 kHz, 3 kHz, and 4 kHz riding along, THD quantifies those uninvited guests. Lower THD means the output is a truer copy of the input.

Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental: the 2nd harmonic is an octave up, the 3rd is an octave plus a fifth, and so on. Every amplifier, speaker, and DAC creates some — the question is only how much, and which orders dominate.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator applies the standard THD definition directly: it takes the RMS voltage of your fundamental signal and the RMS voltages of each harmonic component, combines the harmonics as a root-sum-of-squares, and divides by the fundamental.

Methodology: THD % = √(V₂² + V₃² + V₄² + …) ÷ V₁ × 100, where V₁ is the fundamental and V₂…Vₙ are harmonic RMS voltages. The result is graded: under 0.1% = Reference Grade, under 0.5% = High Fidelity, under 1% = Acceptable, above = High Distortion. THD Formula and Worked Example

THD % = √(V₂² + V₃² + V₄² + …) ÷ V₁ × 100

Worked example: 1 V RMS fundamental with harmonics of 0.02 V, 0.015 V, and 0.01 V:

  1. Square each harmonic: 0.02² = 0.0004; 0.015² = 0.000225; 0.01² = 0.0001
  2. Sum: 0.0004 + 0.000225 + 0.0001 = 0.000725
  3. Square root: √0.000725 = 0.0269 V of distortion
  4. Divide by fundamental: 0.0269 ÷ 1 × 100 = 2.69% THD — high distortion, likely clipping

Now the dB conversion spec sheets love: THD in dB = 20 × log₁₀(THD ÷ 100).

Even vs. Odd Harmonics: Why "Warm" and "Harsh" Exist

Not all distortion sounds the same — which harmonics dominate matters as much as how much. Even-order harmonics (2nd, 4th, 6th) are octaves and consonant intervals of the fundamental; they sound "warm," "full," or "euphonic." This is the famous tube-amplifier sound: single-ended triodes produce predominantly 2nd-harmonic distortion.

Odd-order harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th) create dissonant intervals — the 3rd harmonic sits a twelfth above the fundamental, clashing with the musical harmony. Transistor clipping generates strong odd-order products, which is why a clipping solid-state amp sounds "harsh" or "grainy" while a softly saturating tube amp at the same THD % sounds pleasant. Two devices at 1% THD can sound completely different — the percentage alone never tells the whole story.

When Can You Actually Hear THD?

Under laboratory conditions with pure test tones, trained listeners detect distortion around 1% — and even then mostly in the midrange where hearing is most sensitive. But music is not a test tone: complex signals mask distortion, pushing audibility thresholds several times higher.

Practical takeaways: below 0.1%, THD is inaudible in any real music — the difference between a 0.01% and 0.001% amplifier exists only on the spec sheet. Between 0.5–2%, you might catch harshness on loud, simple passages (solo piano, female vocals). Above ~3–5%, clipping harshness is obvious to anyone. Speaker drivers themselves routinely produce 1–5% THD in the bass at high output — which is why speaker distortion, not amplifier distortion, usually dominates what you hear.

THD vs. THD+N vs. SINAD: The Decoder

Spec sheets use three related numbers — here's how they connect:

MetricWhat it includesRelation
THDHarmonic distortion onlyThe classic percentage
THD+NHarmonics + noise floorAlways ≥ THD; the honest real-world number
SINADSignal ÷ (noise + distortion), in dBSINAD (dB) = −20 × log₁₀(THD+N as a ratio)

A DAC rated "0.0005% THD+N" equals 106 dB SINAD — superb. When comparing gear, match like with like: THD against THD, SINAD against SINAD. And remember noise dominates THD+N at low signal levels, while distortion dominates near maximum output.

Limitations

  • Percentage hides character. 1% of warm 2nd-harmonic and 1% of harsh 3rd-harmonic get the same number — your ears won't.
  • Single-frequency snapshot. Real THD varies with frequency and level; one number can't describe an amplifier's full behavior.
  • No noise included. This is pure THD; real measurements (THD+N) add the noise floor.
  • Ignores intermodulation. IMD (distortion from two interacting tones) often correlates better with perceived harshness than THD.
  • Speaker distortion dominates. Chasing 0.001% amplifier THD is pointless if your speakers produce 2% in the bass — fix the biggest offender first.
