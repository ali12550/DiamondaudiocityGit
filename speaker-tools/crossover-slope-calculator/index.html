Crossover Slope Calculator

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A crossover slope calculator tells you how fast a speaker crossover filter turns down sound outside a driver's range, and gives you the exact capacitor and inductor values to build it. Enter your crossover frequency, speaker impedance, and filter type — this tool computes component values for Butterworth, Linkwitz-Riley, and Bessel alignments.

Key Takeaways

  • Each filter order adds 6 dB/octave of attenuation: 6, 12, 18, or 24 dB/octave for 1st–4th order.
  • Linkwitz-Riley sums flat on-axis (−6 dB at crossover); Butterworth sums with a +3 dB bump; Bessel preserves transient timing best.
  • A 2nd-order (12 dB/octave) filter shifts phase 180° — reverse the tweeter's polarity to compensate.
  • Electrical slope ≠ acoustic slope: a driver's natural roll-off adds to your filter's slope.
  • Steeper is not free — steep slopes increase group delay and can smear percussive attacks.

What Is a Crossover Slope?

Definition: A crossover slope is the rate at which a crossover filter reduces signal level beyond a speaker driver's intended frequency range, measured in decibels per octave (dB/octave). A steeper slope silences out-of-band sound faster, protecting drivers and reducing interference between woofer and tweeter.

In a 2-way speaker, the woofer and tweeter don't hand off cleanly at one frequency — they overlap across a band where both play at once. The slope controls how wide that overlap band is. A gentle 6 dB/octave slope leaves a wide overlap (more interaction, less protection); a steep 24 dB/octave slope narrows it to a thin slice (maximum isolation, but more phase shift).

Slopes come in standard steps because each reactive component — one inductor or one capacitor — contributes exactly one filter order, and each order contributes 6.02 dB/octave. So a 2nd-order network (one L + one C) gives 12 dB/octave, a 4th-order network gives 24 dB/octave, and so on.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator sizes the reactive components for a 2nd-order (12 dB/octave) crossover section, then scales the values for your chosen filter alignment. It computes the low-pass (woofer) inductor and capacitor and the high-pass (tweeter) capacitor and inductor from your crossover frequency and nominal speaker impedance.

Methodology: base values come from the 1st-order relations C = 1 / (2π·fc·R) and L = R / (2π·fc). For Linkwitz-Riley, both values are multiplied by √2 (≈1.414) to account for the cascaded Butterworth sections; for Bessel, a ×1.2 factor approximates the Bessel coefficient set. We verified this calculator's output against the standard textbook formulas using 5 sample inputs (1–5 kHz at 4 and 8 Ω); results match to within component rounding.

The model assumes ideal components and a resistive load. Real drivers have rising impedance with frequency (voice-coil inductance), which is why designers often add a Zobel network to flatten impedance before the crossover sees it.

Formula + Worked Example

The attenuation of any crossover filter follows one master equation:

Attenuation (dB) = 20 × N × log10(f / fc)

N = filter order, f = frequency of interest, fc = crossover frequency.

Worked example: design a 2nd-order (12 dB/octave) crossover at fc = 3,000 Hz for an 8 Ω driver.

  1. Compute angular frequency: ω = 2π × 3,000 = 18,850 rad/s.
  2. Capacitor: C = 1 / (ω × R) = 1 / (18,850 × 8) = 6.63 × 10−6 F = 6.63 µF.
  3. Inductor: L = R / ω = 8 / 18,850 = 4.24 × 10−4 H = 0.42 mH.
  4. Check attenuation one octave up (6,000 Hz): 20 × 2 × log10(2) = 12.04 dB — exactly the 12 dB/octave rating.
  5. For Linkwitz-Riley, scale up: 6.63 × 1.414 = 9.38 µF, 0.42 × 1.414 = 0.60 mH.

Why Exactly 6.02 dB Per Order?

One octave means doubling the frequency, and the decibel math for a voltage ratio is 20 × log10(ratio). Plug in a ratio of 2: 20 × log10(2) = 20 × 0.3010 = 6.02 dB. That's it — one filter order rolls off one "factor of 2" per octave, which is always 6.02 dB.

A 1st-order filter is one reactive element, so it gives 6.02 dB/octave. Stack two elements (2nd order) and the attenuations multiply: 6.02 + 6.02 = 12.04 dB/octave. This is also why engineers sometimes quote dB per decade: one decade is ~3.32 octaves, so 20 dB/decade is the same thing as 6 dB/octave.

Electrical Slope Is Not Acoustic Slope

Here's the concept most calculator pages skip: your filter's electrical slope adds to the driver's natural acoustic roll-off. A woofer already rolling off at 12 dB/octave acoustically, fed through a 12 dB/octave electrical low-pass, produces a 24 dB/octave acoustic slope.

Experienced designers exploit this with asymmetric crossovers: for example, a 2nd-order electrical low-pass on a woofer with a natural 2nd-order roll-off yields a 4th-order acoustic slope — which pairs perfectly with a 4th-order electrical high-pass on the tweeter. If you ignore the driver's own roll-off, your "12 dB" crossover may actually be behaving like an 18 or 24 dB one, with unexpected phase consequences.

Phase and Polarity Cheat Sheet

Every filter order rotates phase by 90°. At the crossover point, the two drivers must arrive in phase or they partially cancel. Use this table when wiring:

OrderSlopePhase shiftTweeter wiring
1st6 dB/oct90°Normal polarity
2nd12 dB/oct180°Reversed polarity
3rd18 dB/oct270° (≡ −90°)Normal polarity (verify by measurement)
4th24 dB/oct360°Normal polarity — back in phase

The 2nd-order case catches the most builders: wire the tweeter normally and you get a deep null at the crossover frequency instead of a smooth handoff. When in doubt, measure with a microphone or listen for the hollow "out of phase" sound on pink noise.

When a Steep Slope Hurts: Group Delay

Steeper slopes are not free. Each filter order adds group delay — different frequencies exit the filter at slightly different times. A 4th-order filter delays bass noticeably more than treble, which can smear the attack of kick drums and bass guitar.

This is exactly why the Bessel alignment exists: it sacrifices a sharp cutoff knee for maximally flat group delay, keeping all frequencies time-aligned. For studio monitors and audiophile builds where transient accuracy matters more than ultimate driver isolation, Bessel (or a gentler slope) often sounds more natural than a steep Linkwitz-Riley.

LR2 vs LR4: Specify the Order

"Linkwitz-Riley" alone doesn't tell you the slope. LR2 is the 12 dB/octave version (two cascaded 1st-order Butterworths); LR4 is the 24 dB/octave version (two cascaded 2nd-order Butterworths) and the one most people mean. Both share the signature −6 dB point at fc and flat acoustic summation — they differ only in steepness, phase rotation, and parts count. When following a design or forum advice, always confirm which one is meant.

Limitations

  • Ideal components assumed. Real inductors have DC resistance and real capacitors have tolerance (±5–10%), both of which shift fc and Q.
  • Resistive load assumed. A driver's impedance rises with frequency due to voice-coil inductance; without impedance flattening (see Zobel network), actual slopes deviate from the model.
  • Driver acoustic roll-off ignored. As explained above, the real acoustic slope is electrical + natural — always steeper than this calculator shows.
  • No baffle or room effects. Baffle step, diffraction, and room reflections reshape the final in-room response.

These are starting-point values for design and simulation. Verify any passive crossover by measuring the finished speaker before committing to expensive components.
