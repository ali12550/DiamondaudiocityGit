What is speaker sensitivity?

Speaker sensitivity is the loudness a speaker produces — in decibels — when fed a standard signal and measured from 1 meter away. It's written as dB SPL at 1W/1m, or sometimes dB at 2.83V/1m. Typical home speakers land between 85 and 92 dB; horn-loaded designs reach 95 dB and up, while some compact sealed speakers sit near 80–84 dB.

Think of sensitivity as the speaker's efficiency at turning electricity into sound. A speaker rated 91 dB needs half the amplifier power of an 88 dB speaker to play equally loud. That 3 dB difference is the single most useful number when you're matching speakers to an amplifier — it decides whether a 30-watt amp is plenty or hopelessly underpowered.

How is sensitivity measured?

Manufacturers measure sensitivity in an anechoic chamber — a room with no reflections — usually with 1 watt (or 2.83 volts) of pink noise or a swept tone, averaged across the midband. The microphone sits exactly 1 meter from the speaker.

That anechoic part matters. Your living room isn't anechoic: walls, floor, and ceiling reflect sound back at you, adding roughly 3–6 dB of room gain in a typical furnished room. So a speaker that measures 88 dB in the lab often delivers closer to 91–94 dB where you sit — the calculator below ignores room gain on purpose, giving you a conservative worst-case number.

How this calculator works

The widget combines three physics facts into one estimate. First, every doubling of amplifier power adds 3 dB: that's the 10 × log₁₀(power) term. Second, sound pressure falls with distance following the inverse square law, so every doubling of distance removes 6 dB: the 20 × log₁₀(distance) term. Third, the speaker's sensitivity is the starting point — what it does with the first watt at one meter.

It then classifies the result two ways. The efficiency class rates the speaker itself: 95 dB or higher is High, 88–94 dB is Moderate, and below 88 dB is Low. The amplifier stress warning fires when you pair a low-sensitivity speaker (under 86 dB) with more than 200 watts — a combination that runs amps hot and flirts with clipping.

The formula (used by the calculator)

SPL = sensitivity + 10 × log₁₀(power) − 20 × log₁₀(distance)

The result is rounded to the nearest whole decibel. The impedance selector (2/4/8 Ω) doesn't change the SPL number — see the FAQ below for why.

Worked example

Take a typical bookshelf speaker rated 88 dB, driven by a 100-watt amp, heard from 3 meters (about 10 feet):

  • Power gain: 10 × log₁₀(100) = +20 dB
  • Distance loss: 20 × log₁₀(3) ≈ −9.5 dB
  • SPL = 88 + 20 − 9.5 = 98.5 → 98 dB

That's roughly the level of a passing motorcycle — plenty for music, and exactly what the widget shows for these inputs.

How to use the calculator

  1. Speaker sensitivity: find the dB rating on the spec sheet or the back of the speaker (80–110 dB). Check whether it's rated at 1W or 2.83V — the FAQ explains the trap.
  2. Amplifier power: enter your amp's RMS watts per channel, not peak watts. Peak numbers inflate the result by 3 dB or more.
  3. Listening distance: measure from the speaker to your head in meters, not the room's diagonal.
  4. Impedance: select your speaker's nominal impedance for reference. It doesn't alter the SPL math here.
  5. Read the estimated SPL, the efficiency class, and the amplifier stress indicator.

How many watts do you actually need?

Work backwards from a target. For comfortable home listening you want peaks around 95–100 dB at your seat; home theater reference peaks hit about 105 dB. Rearranging the formula: required watts = 10^((target − sensitivity + 20 × log₁₀(distance)) / 10).

Example: 90 dB speakers, 2.5-meter seat, 100 dB target → 10^((100 − 90 + 7.96) / 10) ≈ 62 watts. So a 75–100 W amp gives comfortable headroom. Do the same math with 84 dB speakers and you'd need nearly 250 watts — that's the price of low sensitivity, paid in amplifier.

Common mistakes

  • The 2.83V trap: 2.83 volts into 8 Ω is exactly 1 watt — but into 4 Ω it's 2 watts, which reads 3 dB louder. A 4-Ω speaker rated "90 dB at 2.83V" is really an 87 dB speaker at 1 watt. Always check which standard the spec uses before comparing brands.
  • Using peak watts: "200W peak" often means 100W RMS. Entering peak power inflates your SPL estimate.
  • Ignoring distance: moving from 2 m to 4 m costs you 6 dB — equivalent to quadrupling your amp. Measure your actual seat.
  • Chasing watts instead of sensitivity: upgrading from an 86 dB to a 92 dB speaker buys you 6 dB — the same as quadrupling amplifier power, usually for less money.
  • Forgetting headroom: music peaks run 10–20 dB above the average. An amp sized exactly for your target SPL will clip on loud passages; leave 3–6 dB of headroom.
