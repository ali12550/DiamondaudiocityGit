    What Is a Ported Enclosure?

    Definition: A ported (bass reflex) enclosure is a speaker box with a tuned vent — a tube or slot — that forms a Helmholtz resonator with the box air volume. At the tuning frequency (Fb), the air mass in the port resonates, reinforcing bass output by 3–6 dB below what a sealed box of the same size produces, while the driver's cone excursion drops to nearly zero.

    Think of blowing across a bottle top: the air in the neck bounces on the spring of the air inside. A ported box works the same way — the port's air mass and the box's air volume form a resonator tuned to Fb. Near Fb the port does the heavy lifting, which is why ported subs play deeper and louder than sealed equivalents from the same driver.

    The trade: below Fb the resonator stops helping and the driver unloads — excursion rises steeply, risking damage at high power. Ported boxes also ring slightly longer near tuning (higher group delay) than sealed boxes.

    How This Calculator Works

    This calculator solves the Helmholtz-based port length formula for your box volume and target tuning, then derives vent area and a rough air-velocity estimate. Round ports use the true diameter; slot ports are converted via an equivalent diameter so the same physics applies.

    Methodology: the widget implements Lv = (2100 × D² × N) / (Fb² × Vb) − 0.732 × D, with D in inches, Vb in cubic feet, Fb in Hz; 0.732 is the flanged end-correction factor (see below). Slot ports convert via equivalent diameter Deq = 2√(A/π). Air velocity is a built-in rough estimate that flags above 25 m/s. See Limitations for an important accuracy note on the length output.

    Formula + Worked Example

    Lv = (2100 × D² × N) / (Fb² × Vb) − 0.732 × D

    Lv, D in inches · Vb in ft³ · Fb in Hz · N = number of ports.

    Worked example: 2.0 ft³ box tuned to Fb = 35 Hz with one 4-inch round port, using the Helmholtz-derived imperial form Lv = (2100 × D² × N) / (Fb² × Vb) − 0.732 × D:

    1. Numerator: 2100 × 4² × 1 = 2100 × 16 = 33,600.
    2. Denominator: 35² × 2.0 = 1225 × 2 = 2,450.
    3. Divide: 33,600 / 2,450 = 13.71.
    4. Subtract end correction: 13.71 − 0.732 × 4 = 13.71 − 2.93 = ≈ 10.8 inches.

    The relationships to internalize: lower tuning needs a longer port, bigger diameter needs a longer port, and bigger box needs a shorter port. (Note: the widget's built-in constant differs — see Limitations.)

    The 0.732 in the Formula: End Correction Explained

    A port behaves longer than its physical length because the air just outside the mouth oscillates with the air inside — like the water sloshing past a harbor entrance. This end correction adds roughly 0.6 diameters for a free (unflanged) end and about 0.85 for a flanged end; the widget's 0.732 factor is the standard compromise for a typical flanged tube port.

    Why it matters: ignore end correction and your box tunes higher than intended — a 35 Hz design can land at 38 Hz, audibly shifting the bass character. Slot ports that share a box wall get extra effective length from the wall itself, which is one reason slot-ported boxes often measure slightly lower than the raw math predicts.

    Port Noise: The 17 m/s Rule

    Chuffing — the puffing, whistling noise from a port at high output — is air turbulence at the port mouth. It starts when air velocity exceeds roughly 5% of the speed of sound (0.05 × 343 ≈ 17 m/s). Below that, flow stays smooth and silent; above it, turbulence grows fast.

    Three fixes, in order of effectiveness: enlarge the port area (velocity drops with area), flare the ends (aeroports with flared mouths tolerate ~25–30 m/s), and lower expectations — a subwoofer at full tilt in a car tolerates more noise than a hi-fi sub in a quiet room. The widget's 25 m/s warning is the "definitely fix this" line.

    Below Fb the Driver Unloads — Use a Subsonic Filter

    Above tuning, the port's air mass controls the cone. Below Fb, that control vanishes — the driver behaves as if in free air, and excursion climbs steeply with falling frequency. A 30 Hz-tuned sub playing 20 Hz content at high power can bottom out the voice coil.

    The fix is a subsonic (infrasonic) filter: a high-pass set just below Fb (typically 3–5 Hz under tuning, 12–24 dB/octave). It costs nothing audible — there's little musical content down there anyway — and it converts a blown-driver warranty claim into a non-event. Any ported subwoofer driven hard without one is living dangerously.

    Net Volume: Subtract Before You Calculate

    The formula wants net air volume — what remains after the driver basket, the port tube itself, and internal bracing. Worked example: a box with 2.0 ft³ gross internal volume, a 12" driver displacing 0.15 ft³, a 4"×14" port displacing ~0.10 ft³, and bracing at 0.05 ft³ nets 1.70 ft³. Designing for 2.0 ft³ when you really have 1.7 tunes the box nearly 10% sharp.

    Always work backwards: pick target net volume → add displacements → cut wood for the gross volume. The port's own displacement is the one builders most often forget — and it's the largest of the three.

    Limitations

    • End-correction is approximate. The widget uses the standard flanged end-correction (0.732 × D); free-end or flared ports shift the effective length slightly — measure twice, cut once.
    • Ideal Helmholtz model. Real boxes have leakage (QL), absorption, and panel flex that shift Fb a few percent from the math.
    • Velocity is a rough estimate. The widget's approximation assumes ~1 W drive; real velocity scales with actual power and program material.
    • No driver parameters. Vas, Fs, and Qts determine whether a driver suits a ported box at all — the calculator sizes the port, not the alignment.
    • End correction is approximate. Flared aeroports and shared-wall slots deviate from the 0.732 factor; verify critical builds by measuring the impedance valley.

    Use these numbers to cut wood with confidence, then verify Fb with an impedance measurement of the finished box.
