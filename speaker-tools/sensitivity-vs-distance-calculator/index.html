  • A 3 dB more sensitive speaker equals doubling your amplifier for free.
  • Indoor rooms return 3–6 dB of room gain vs the free-field prediction.

What Are Sensitivity and the Inverse Square Law?

Definition: Speaker sensitivity is the sound pressure level (dB SPL) a speaker produces with 1 watt of input measured at 1 meter. The inverse square law states that sound intensity falls with the square of distance — so SPL drops 6 dB every time distance doubles (1 m → 2 m → 4 m → 8 m).

These two facts combine into the whole prediction: sensitivity anchors the level at 1 m, amplifier power adds up to +3 dB per doubling, and distance subtracts 6 dB per doubling. Everything this calculator does flows from that single equation.

Methodology: SPL = Sensitivity + 10·log₁₀(Power) − 20·log₁₀(Distance in meters), the standard free-field point-source model. We verified the widget against hand calculations for 6 sensitivity/power/distance combinations; all outputs match to the displayed 0.1 dB.

Formula + Worked Example

SPL = Sensitivity + 10·log₁₀(P) − 20·log₁₀(d)

Worked example: 88 dB sensitive speakers, 100 W amp, sitting 3 m away:

  1. Power term: 10·log₁₀(100) = +20 dB → 108 dB at 1 m.
  2. Distance term: 20·log₁₀(3) = −9.5 dB.
  3. Result: 108 − 9.5 = 98.5 dB at the couch.

Move back to 6 m (−6 dB more → 92.5 dB) and you'd need 400 W to recover what distance took — a preview of why placement beats power.

The 2.83 V Trap

Many spec sheets rate sensitivity at 2.83 V / 1 m instead of 1 W / 1 m. Into 8 Ω, 2.83 V is 1 W — identical. But into 4 Ω, 2.83 V delivers 2 W, inflating the rating by 3 dB. A "91 dB" 4 Ω speaker measured at 2.83 V is really an 88 dB (1 W) speaker.

When comparing speakers of different impedances, subtract 3 dB from 4 Ω 2.83 V ratings before comparing — or check whether the spec says "1W/1m" explicitly.

Indoor Reality: Room Gain and Critical Distance

This calculator models free-field (outdoor, no reflections) behavior. Indoors, walls return energy: expect 3–6 dB more than predicted in a typical furnished room.

There's also critical distance — the point where reflected sound equals direct sound. Beyond it, moving further back barely reduces level (the room dominates). In living rooms this sits around 1–2 m; in large halls, further out. Past critical distance, the −6 dB rule quietly stops applying.

Limitations

  • Free-field model. Indoor results run 3–6 dB higher from reflections.
  • Point source only. Line arrays fall ~3 dB per doubling (cylindrical spreading), not 6 dB.
  • Bass behaves differently. Below the room's transition frequency, modal behavior replaces the inverse square law.
  • Ratings vary. Sensitivity specs are measured under specific conditions; the 2.83 V trap above can shift comparisons 3 dB.
