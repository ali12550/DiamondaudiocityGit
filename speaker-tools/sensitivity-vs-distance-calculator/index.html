Acoustic Propagation Physics: Inverse Square Law & Distance Loss

Predicting how loud a loudspeaker will sound at a listener's ears requires bridging two distinct physical relationships: electrical power conversion (sensitivity to 1-meter acoustic output) and acoustic wavefront propagation through three-dimensional space.

Loudspeaker sensitivity describes the acoustic efficiency of converting electrical voltage and current into mechanical cone motion. In anechoic free space (free field), as that acoustic pressure travels away from the transducer, the acoustic energy expands across an ever-larger surface area, reducing the sound pressure level arriving at the ear.

The Mathematical Formulation

The combined transfer function modeling acoustic sound pressure level at distance $r$ under electrical drive power $P$ is given by:

$$ ext{SPL}(r) = ext{Sensitivity} + 10 log_{10}(P) - 20 log_{10}(r) quad [ ext{dB SPL}]$$
Where $ ext{Sensitivity}$ is measured in dB at 1 watt at 1 meter, $P$ is electrical amplifier power in Watts RMS, and $r$ is axial distance in meters.
Power Gain Term:
$$Delta ext{SPL}_{ ext{power}} = 10 log_{10}(P)$$
Every doubling of amplifier wattage adds +3.01 dB.
Distance Attenuation Term:
$$Delta ext{SPL}_{ ext{dist}} = -20 log_{10}left( rac{r_2}{r_1} ight)$$
Every doubling of distance reduces SPL by -6.02 dB.

Point Source vs. Line Source Propagation

The geometry of a loudspeaker transducer array determines how wavefronts expand through space:

Transducer Source Geometry Wavefront Shape Attenuation Rate per Distance Doubling Acoustic Application
Point Source (Conventional Box) Spherical ($A = 4pi r^2$) -6.0 dB / doubling ($1/r^2$ intensity) Studio monitors, home hi-fi speakers, domestic home theaters.
Line Source (Concert Line Array) Cylindrical ($A = 2pi r h$) -3.0 dB / doubling ($1/r$ intensity in near field) Stadium line arrays, vertical column speakers, tall planar ribbon transducers.

Real-World In-Room Acoustics: The Critical Distance ($D_c$)

The theoretical $-6, ext{dB}$ drop per doubling strictly applies outdoors or inside an anechoic chamber. Inside domestic living rooms, auditoriums, and clubs, boundary reflections retain acoustic energy within the room:

$$D_c approx 0.057 sqrt{ rac{Q cdot V}{T_{60}}} quad [ ext{meters}]$$
Where $Q$ is speaker directivity factor, $V$ is room volume in $ ext{m}^3$, and $T_{60}$ is reverberation time in seconds.
  • Within Critical Distance ($r < D_c$): Direct sound dominates. SPL decays at approximately -4.5 dB to -6 dB per distance doubling. Stereo imaging is crisp and direct.
  • Beyond Critical Distance ($r > D_c$): Diffuse reverberant reflections dominate. The sound level plateaus into a reverberant sound field that decays by only -1.5 dB to -3 dB per doubling of distance.

Frequently Asked Questions

In a free-field acoustic environment without reflective boundaries, sound radiates outward from a point source as an expanding spherical wavefront. The surface area of a sphere is proportional to the square of its radius ($A = 4pi r^2$). Because total acoustic power remains constant, acoustic intensity drops with the square of distance ($1/r^2$), which translates mathematically to a 6.02 dB drop per doubling of distance ($20 log_{10}(d_2 / d_1)$).

The critical distance ($D_c$) is the boundary location where the direct acoustic sound pressure from the speaker equals the diffuse reverberant sound pressure reflected from the walls, floor, and ceiling. Closer than $D_c$, the direct wave dominates and sound drops at nearly -6 dB per doubling. Beyond $D_c$, the reverberant sound field dominates, causing SPL to level off to a near-constant baseline that decays by only 2 to 3 dB per doubling.

A standard point source radiates a spherical wavefront and drops at 6 dB per doubling of distance. A line array (a tall vertical column of closely spaced drivers) couples acoustically to produce a cylindrical wavefront in its near field. Cylindrical wavefronts expand over area $A = 2pi r h$, resulting in an intensity drop of only $1/r$, or 3 dB per doubling of distance. This allows line arrays to throw intelligible sound much farther in auditoriums and outdoor concerts.

Doubling distance from 1 meter to 2 meters causes a 6 dB drop, and doubling again to 4 meters causes another 6 dB drop, for a total loss of 12 dB. Because every 3 dB increase in acoustic output requires doubling electrical amplifier wattage ($10^{12/10} = 15.85$), an amplifier must supply approximately 16 times more power to maintain the exact same SPL at 4 meters as it produced at 1 meter.

For a standard 8-ohm nominal speaker, 2.83 volts RMS delivers exactly 1 watt of power ($P = V^2 / R = 2.83^2 / 8 = 1.0, ext{W}$), meaning the two ratings are identical. However, for a 4-ohm speaker, 2.83 volts delivers 2 watts of power ($2.83^2 / 4 = 2.0, ext{W}$). Consequently, a 4-ohm speaker rated at 2.83V/1m will measure 3 dB higher than its true 1W/1m sensitivity.

Adding a second identical loudspeaker playing the same coherent audio signal increases acoustic output by +3 dB if powered separately with the same total wattage, or by +6 dB (mutual acoustic coupling) if both drivers are positioned within 1/4 wavelength of each other and each receives full amplifier power. Subjectively, a 10 dB increase is perceived by the human ear as a doubling of loudness.
