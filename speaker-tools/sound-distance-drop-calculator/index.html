The Physics of Sound Attenuation Over Distance

When an acoustic source generates sound, it injects mechanical vibrational power into the surrounding air. In an ideal unobstructed environment, this acoustic power radiates outward as an expanding spherical bubble. Because the surface area of a sphere grows proportionally to the square of its radius ($A = 4pi r^2$), the fixed acoustic power must spread across an ever-expanding geometric wavefront, diluting the acoustic energy density.

This phenomenon is codified as the Inverse Square Law. Understanding how distance attenuation interacts with indoor reverberation and atmospheric absorption is critical for sound reinforcement, concert production, studio mixing, and architectural noise control.

The Inverse Square Law Formulation

Acoustic intensity $I$ (power per unit area, $ ext{W/m}^2$) at distance $r$ from an omnidirectional point source radiating total sound power $W$ is:

$$I = rac{W}{4pi r^2} implies rac{I_2}{I_1} = left( rac{r_1}{r_2} ight)^2$$

Because human hearing perceives loudness logarithmically, acoustic decibels relate directly to the ratio of sound pressures ($p propto sqrt{I}$):

$$Delta ext{SPL} = 20 log_{10}left( rac{r_2}{r_1} ight) quad [ ext{dB}]$$

When distance doubles ($r_2 = 2 cdot r_1$):

$$Delta ext{SPL} = 20 log_{10}(2) approx 20 imes 0.30103 = -6.02, ext{dB}$$

Indoor vs. Outdoor Propagation: The Critical Distance ($D_c$)

The strict $-6.02, ext{dB}$ per doubling rule applies exclusively in outdoor free-field or anechoic chamber environments. Inside rooms, reflections fundamentally alter sound decay:

Acoustic Environment Boundary Condition Decay Rate per Distance Doubling Primary Physical Driver
Free Field (4π Space) Open air, elevated above ground -6.02 dB / doubling Pure spherical wave expansion; zero boundary reflections.
Hemispherical (2π Space) Loudspeaker placed on outdoor ground -5.7 dB to -6.0 dB / doubling Half-space radiation; ground reflections boost forward SPL by +3 dB.
Enclosed Room (Near Field, $r < D_c$) Between speaker and critical distance -4.0 dB to -5.5 dB / doubling Direct wave dominates over early boundary reflections.
Enclosed Room (Reverberant Field, $r > D_c$) Beyond critical distance in hall/room -1.5 dB to -3.0 dB / doubling Diffuse reverberant sound field plateaus; sound energy bounces continuously.

Atmospheric Air Absorption at Long Distances (ISO 9613-1)

At distances exceeding 50 to 100 meters (such as outdoor festivals or stadium PA systems), sound attenuation is no longer purely geometric. Viscous friction and molecular thermal relaxation of diatomic nitrogen ($N_2$) and oxygen ($O_2$) molecules extract acoustic energy directly from the passing wave:

$$ ext{SPL}(r) = ext{SPL}_0 - 20 log_{10}left( rac{r}{r_0} ight) - alpha_{ ext{atm}} cdot rac{r - r_0}{100}$$
Where $alpha_{ ext{atm}}$ is the atmospheric absorption coefficient in dB per 100 meters, heavily dependent on frequency, humidity, and temperature.
  • Bass Frequencies (100 Hz): Air absorption is negligible ($approx 0.1, ext{dB}/100 ext{m}$). Bass travels miles with minimal air friction.
  • High Frequencies (8 kHz – 10 kHz): Air absorption can reach 5 dB to 15 dB per 100 meters at 20°C and 20% relative humidity. This severe high-frequency damping causes distant concerts to sound boomy and muffled unless high-frequency line array horns are boosted to compensate.

Psychoacoustics: Decibel Drop vs. Subjective Loudness

Human hearing does not perceive volume linearly. According to Stevens' Power Law and Fletcher-Munson equal-loudness contours:

  • -1 dB Drop: Just Noticeable Difference (JND) under ideal studio monitoring conditions.
  • -3 dB Drop: Clearly perceptible change in volume; corresponds to a 50% loss of electrical amplifier wattage.
  • -6 dB Drop: Halving of physical acoustic sound pressure ($p_2 = 0.5 cdot p_1$); perceived as roughly 35% quieter.
  • -10 dB Drop: Subjective halving of perceived loudness ("half as loud"). Corresponds to 90% loss of acoustic sound energy.
  • -20 dB Drop: Subjective one-quarter ($1/4$) loudness. Occurs at $10 imes$ distance in free field.

Frequently Asked Questions

The Inverse Square Law states that sound intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from a point source ($I propto 1/r^2$). Because acoustic sound pressure level (SPL) is logarithmic, doubling the distance from the source decreases SPL by 6.02 dB: $Delta ext{SPL} = 20 cdot log_{10}(d_2 / d_1)$.

Outdoors, sound radiates into infinite space without returning. Indoors, architectural boundaries (walls, floor, ceiling) reflect acoustic waves back into the room, creating a diffuse reverberant field. Once a listener moves beyond the critical distance ($D_c$), the reverberant sound energy exceeds direct sound, slowing distance attenuation to only -2 dB to -4 dB per doubling of distance.

According to psychoacoustic research (Stevens' Power Law), a 1 dB change is the smallest threshold detectable under laboratory conditions, a 3 dB change is clearly noticeable, a 6 dB drop represents a halving of acoustic sound pressure (perceived as roughly 35% quieter), and a 10 dB drop is perceived subjectively by human hearing as cutting perceived loudness exactly in half.

In addition to geometric spreading loss, sound traveling through air suffers viscous and molecular thermal relaxation losses (ISO 9613-1). At distances exceeding 50 to 100 meters, frequencies above 4 kHz suffer additional attenuation of 2 dB to 10 dB per 100 meters depending on ambient air temperature and relative humidity, causing sound to become muffled at long distances.

In a free field (4π space), sound radiates in all directions as a full sphere. When a loudspeaker or subwoofer is positioned on the ground outdoors (hemispherical 2π space), the ground reflects sound forward in-phase with the direct wave, increasing acoustic output by +3 dB to +6 dB compared to free-field space while still obeying inverse square spreading.

Increasing amplifier wattage provides only limited compensation. Because distance loss is logarithmic, recovering 6 dB lost by doubling distance requires a 4-fold increase in amplifier wattage (e.g. 250W becomes 1000W). In large concert venues, deploying delayed satellite speakers or vertical line arrays is vastly more energy-efficient and acoustically effective than simply cranking amplifier power.
