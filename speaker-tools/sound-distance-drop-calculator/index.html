What Is Sound Distance Drop?

Sound distance drop is the loss of sound pressure level (SPL) that happens as you move away from a sound source. Outdoors, it follows the inverse square law: every doubling of distance cuts SPL by about 6 dB, because the same acoustic power spreads over four times the wavefront area.

Definition: Sound distance drop is the reduction in decibels of sound pressure level between two distances from a source, caused mainly by geometric spreading of the sound wave, plus air absorption and room reflections depending on the environment. It is calculated as 20 times the base-10 logarithm of the distance ratio.

Key Takeaways

  • Double the distance, lose 6 dB — outdoors in a free field, 100 dB at 1 m becomes ~94 dB at 2 m and ~88 dB at 4 m.
  • Indoors the rule breaks down — past the room's critical distance, reflections take over and loss slows to ~2–4 dB per doubling.
  • Recovering 6 dB costs 4× the amplifier power — moving speakers closer (or adding delay speakers) beats buying bigger amps.
  • −10 dB sounds about half as loud to human hearing, even though it is a 90% loss of acoustic energy.
  • High frequencies die first outdoors — air absorption (ISO 9613-1) muffles treble over long distances while bass carries on.

Formula and Worked Example

The core formula converts any distance change into decibels of loss:

ΔSPL = 20 · log₁₀(d₂ / d₁)  [dB]

Worked example (free field): a PA speaker measures 100 dB at 1 m. What reaches a listener at 32 m?

  1. Distance ratio: 32 ÷ 1 = 32
  2. log₁₀(32) = 1.505
  3. Drop: 20 × 1.505 = 30.1 dB
  4. Result: 100 − 30.1 = ≈ 70 dB SPL — about as loud as a vacuum cleaner.

Same setup, three environments (this calculator's models): free field → 69.9 dB; on an outdoor ground plane → 71.4 dB; enclosed indoor → 78.9 dB. The room's reflections rescue nearly 9 dB at 32 m — which is exactly why indoor and outdoor system design differ so much.

Beyond the 6 dB Rule: Pro Techniques

The 6 dB rule is the starting point. These four techniques are what working sound engineers actually use — and what most calculator pages never explain.

Find your room's critical distance

Critical distance (Dc) is the distance from the source where direct sound and reverberant sound are equally loud. Inside Dc the 6 dB rule roughly holds; beyond it, room reflections dominate and level barely falls.

Dc ≈ 0.057 · √(V / RT₆₀), with room volume V in m³ and reverberation time RT₆₀ in seconds (omnidirectional source). Worked example: a 200 m³ hall with RT₆₀ of 1.2 s → Dc = 0.057 · √(200/1.2) = 0.057 · 12.9 ≈ 0.74 m. In a lively hall you are in the reverberant field almost everywhere past arm's length — which is why our calculator's indoor model drops far slower than free field. Watts needed to recover lost dB

Amplifier power buys decibels logarithmically: every 3 dB costs double the watts. Use this table before buying a bigger amp to "throw farther":

Recovering a single doubling of distance (6 dB) quadruples your power bill and heat. A more sensitive speaker or a closer/delayed speaker almost always wins. Check your amp choice with our Amplifier Power Calculator.

Why distant sound turns muffled: air absorption

Atmospheric absorption (ISO 9613-1) steals extra decibels as sound travels through air — and it hits high frequencies hardest. Past ~50–100 m this matters as much as geometric spreading:

FrequencyExtra loss per 100 m (typical)What you hear far away
100 Hz (bass)≈ 0.1 dBBass carries for hundreds of meters
1 kHz (mids)≈ 0.5–1 dBSpeech stays intelligible
4 kHz (presence)≈ 2–4 dBClarity starts to dull
8–10 kHz (treble)≈ 5–15 dBCymbals and "air" vanish — the muffled-concert effect

Values depend on temperature and humidity (dry air absorbs more). This is why festival engineers boost high-frequency horns on far-throw line arrays.

Delay-speaker math for venues

Instead of fighting distance loss with watts, large venues place delay towers partway to the audience and delay their signal so sound arrives in sync. Sound travels ≈ 1,125 ft/s, so allow ≈ 0.9 ms of delay per foot (≈ 2.9 ms per meter) of tower distance.

Worked example: delay tower 100 ft from the main PA → 100 × 0.9 ≈ 89 ms of delay. Set it on the system processor and the tower reinforces instead of smearing the sound. For coverage planning, pair this with our Speaker Placement Calculator.

Limitations

  • Point-source assumption. The formula assumes sound radiates equally in all directions. Line arrays and horn-loaded speakers are directional — their real-world drop differs, especially in the near field.
  • Indoor model is simplified. The widget's 0.7 environment factor approximates average room behavior; real rooms have a distinct critical distance (see above) with near-field and reverberant-field regions.
  • No frequency dependence. The calculator gives one broadband number; as the air-absorption table shows, treble drops faster than bass over long distances.
  • No wind, temperature gradients, or obstacles. Outdoors, wind and temperature inversions can bend sound and change levels by several dB.
  • Ground plane is idealized. Real ground absorbs some energy; the +3 dB ground boost assumes a hard reflective surface.

Meters, Feet, and dB

The widget uses meters; 1 m ≈ 3.28 ft, so multiply feet by 0.305 to convert. The formula only cares about the ratio of the two distances, so any consistent unit works. Results are in dB SPL (referenced to 20 µPa) — the same unit on any sound level meter you would verify with.
