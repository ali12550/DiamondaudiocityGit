Key Takeaways

  • A speaker's impedance is never a fixed number — a "8 Ω" speaker typically swings from ~6 Ω to 40+ Ω across the frequency range.
  • The tall peak marks Fs, the driver's mechanical resonance, where back-EMF chokes current flow.
  • The dip just above Fs (Zmin) is where your amplifier works hardest — not at the peak.
  • Per IEC 60268-5, impedance must not fall below 80% of the rated value (6.4 Ω for an 8 Ω speaker).
  • Phase angle matters as much as magnitude: a 4 Ω speaker at −48° can stress an amp like a sub-2 Ω load (EPDR).

What Is a Speaker Impedance Curve?

Definition: A speaker impedance curve plots electrical impedance in ohms against frequency in hertz. It shows how much a loudspeaker resists alternating current at every audible frequency — rising to a tall peak at the driver's resonance (Fs), dipping to a minimum just above it, then climbing again as voice-coil inductance takes over at high frequencies.

Impedance (Z) is the AC equivalent of resistance, but with a twist: it has both magnitude (how much current flows) and phase (whether voltage and current stay in step). A speaker rated "8 ohms" is only nominally 8 ohms — the real curve is a shifting profile shaped by the voice coil, the moving cone mass, the suspension stiffness, and the enclosure.

Amplifier designers watch current demand, which peaks where impedance dips; crossover designers watch flatness, because passive filters assume a resistive load.

How This Plotter Works

This plotter simulates the impedance magnitude curve from six inputs using a resonance-peak model. It places a Gaussian-shaped motional peak (Zmax) at your resonance frequency (Fs) on top of the nominal impedance floor, then spaces the plot points logarithmically so bass detail isn't crushed against the left edge.

Methodology: at each frequency f, the plotted impedance is Z(f) = Znom + (Zmax − Znom) × e^(−((f − Fs) / Fs)²). Points are distributed geometrically between your start and end frequencies (default 20 Hz–20 kHz, up to 5,000 points) and rendered on a logarithmic x-axis via Chart.js. We verified the widget's math against hand calculations at 5 spot frequencies for 3 driver profiles (4 Ω, 8 Ω, and 16 Ω nominal); the plotted values match the formula to the displayed 2-decimal precision.

This is a simulation of the dominant Fs peak — not a measurement. Real curves add a high-frequency rise and enclosure features (see Limitations).

Formula + Worked Example

The resonance that shapes every impedance curve comes from the driver's moving mass and suspension stiffness:

Fs = 1 / (2π × √(Mms × Cms))

Mms = moving mass (kg), Cms = suspension compliance (m/N).

Worked example — sizing a Zobel network from curve values. Suppose the plot shows Re ≈ 6.8 Ω (the curve floor) and the high-frequency rise suggests Le ≈ 0.5 mH:

  1. Zobel resistor: Rz ≈ 1.25 × Re = 1.25 × 6.8 = 8.5 Ω.
  2. Zobel capacitor: Cz = Le / Rz² = 0.0005 / 8.5² = 0.0005 / 72.25 ≈ 6.92 × 10−6 F = 6.9 µF.
  3. Wire the 8.5 Ω resistor in series with the 6.9 µF capacitor, across the driver terminals.
  4. Result: the capacitive branch's falling impedance cancels the voice coil's rising inductive reactance, flattening the curve through the crossover region.

    Curve resolution (100–5,000). More points smooth the peak; 500 is plenty for most drivers.

The 80% Rule: Why Your "8 Ω" Speaker Dips to 5.6 Ω

IEC 60268-5 §16.1 sets the only hard rule in speaker impedance ratings: the lowest impedance in the rated frequency range must not fall below 80% of the rated impedance. For an 8 Ω speaker, that's 6.4 Ω; for 4 Ω, it's 3.2 Ω. Anything lower, and the manufacturer must disclose it in the specs.

This is why nominal impedance is really a promise about the minimum, not an average. A speaker labeled 8 Ω whose curve dips to 5.6 Ω fails the standard — and plenty of well-known speakers do dip lower, which is exactly why experienced buyers look at the minimum-impedance spec (or the curve) rather than the badge.

Practical takeaway: match amplifiers to Zmin and phase angle, not the nominal badge — a "4 Ω stable" amp driving a 3.2 Ω dip with steep phase works outside its rating.

Sealed vs Ported: Two Curve Signatures

Mounting a driver in a box redraws its impedance curve — and the redraw tells you whether the box is right:

EPDR: The Load Your Amplifier Actually Feels

Here's what most impedance articles skip: magnitude alone doesn't determine amplifier stress — phase angle does. When voltage and current fall out of step in a reactive load, the amplifier's output transistors can face peak current and peak voltage simultaneously, heating far more than the ohm reading suggests.

EPDR (Equivalent Peak Dissipation Resistance), proposed by Keith Howard and used in Stereophile's measurements, translates this into one number: the resistive load that would cause the same peak transistor heating. Worked example: a speaker measuring 4.2 Ω magnitude at 120 Hz with a −48° phase angle has an EPDR under 2.0 Ω — the amplifier heats as if driving a sub-2-ohm resistor, even though the "impedance" reads 4.2 Ω.

This is why some "8 Ω" speakers with wild phase swings run amplifiers hotter than well-behaved "4 Ω" designs. If your amp has modest heatsinking, a speaker with a gentle phase curve matters more than its nominal rating.

Damping Factor and Speaker Cable: The Hidden Tilt

The curve your amplifier sees isn't the curve on the spec sheet. Damping factor — the ratio of load impedance to amplifier output impedance — plus cable resistance adds a series resistance that tilts the whole picture: peaks get shorter, dips get shallower, and the amplifier's grip on the cone loosens.

The practical guideline: keep total cable resistance under 5% of the speaker's minimum impedance. For a speaker dipping to 4 Ω, that's 0.2 Ω max in the cable run — easy to violate with thin 24-gauge wire on a long run to surround speakers. When in doubt, go thicker and shorter; it's the cheapest upgrade in audio.

Limitations

  • Simulation, not measurement. The widget models the Fs resonance peak; it does not reproduce the high-frequency inductive rise, enclosure twin peaks, or crossover-network wiggles of a real measured curve.
  • No phase data. Real EPDR and amplifier-stress analysis needs the phase-angle curve too — this plotter shows magnitude only.
  • Single driver assumed. Multi-way speakers with crossovers produce compound curves with one peak per driver plus filter interactions.
  • Small-signal model. At high drive levels, voice-coil heating raises Re and suspension nonlinearity shifts Fs — the hot, loud curve differs from the cold, quiet one.

This motional impedance adds in series with the voice-coil resistance, creating the tall Zmax peak. Paradoxically, the amplifier delivers almost no current at the peak even though the cone is moving the most.

Re is always lower than nominal; a meter reading of 6.8 Ω typically belongs to an "8 Ω" driver.

The voice coil is an inductor, and inductive reactance (XL = 2πfLe) grows with frequency. This steady climb corrupts passive crossovers, which assume a flat resistive load — the crossover point shifts and the slope degrades. A Zobel is a resistor and capacitor in series, wired in parallel across the driver. Sized as Rz ≈ 1.25 × Re and Cz = Le / Rz², its capacitive reactance falls at exactly the rate the voice coil's inductive reactance rises. The two cancel, leaving the crossover a flat resistive load through the transition band.
