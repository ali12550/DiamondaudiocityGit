Acoustic Suspension Physics & Sealed Loudspeaker Design

A sealed loudspeaker enclosure (also known as an acoustic suspension or closed-box design) isolates the radiation of the driver's rear wave from its front wave by trapping the rear acoustic output inside an airtight cabinet. Unlike bass reflex enclosures that rely on resonant air ports, a sealed box uses the trapped pneumatic volume of air as an acoustic restoring spring.

Because the air trapped inside a sealed cabinet compresses and decompresses with near-perfect linearity, acoustic suspension enclosures exhibit superior transient impulse response, lower group delay, and a gentle 12 dB/octave roll-off that seamlessly integrates with domestic room gain and vehicle cabin gain.

The Thiele-Small Closed-Box Formulation

When a raw loudspeaker driver is installed into an airtight enclosure of net volume $V_b$, the pneumatic compliance of the box air ($C_{ab}$) adds in parallel with the driver's mechanical suspension compliance ($C_{ms}$), increasing the total system stiffness. The fundamental closed-box design equations derived by Richard Small and Neville Thiele govern this interaction:

Required Net Box Volume ($V_b$):
$$V_b = rac{V_{as}}{left( rac{Q_{tc}}{Q_{ts}} ight)^2 - 1}$$
System Resonant Frequency ($F_c$):
$$F_c = F_s cdot left( rac{Q_{tc}}{Q_{ts}} ight) = F_s sqrt{1 + rac{V_{as}}{V_b}}$$

Where $V_{as}$ is the driver's equivalent compliance volume, $F_s$ is free-air fundamental resonance, $Q_{ts}$ is total driver quality factor, and $Q_{tc}$ is the target closed-box system damping factor.

Damping Factor Alignments ($Q_{tc}$) Compared

The system $Q_{tc}$ determines the frequency response curve, damping ratio, and time-domain transient ringing of the subwoofer:

Target $Q_{tc}$ Alignment Name Transient Behavior & Step Response Acoustic Character & Application
0.500 Critically Damped Zero transient ringing; fastest impulse decay time. Extremely tight, dry, analytical bass. Requires largest cabinet volume.
0.577 Bessel Alignment Maximally flat group delay and linear phase response. Audiophile music accuracy; uncolored upright bass and kick drums.
0.707 Butterworth (B2) Maximally flat magnitude response down to $F_3$ ($F_3 = F_c$). The gold standard. Best balance between extension, box size, and transient punch.
0.85 – 0.95 Chebyshev / Car Audio Mild +1.0 dB to +1.5 dB peak before roll-off; slight transient ringing. Compact car audio subwoofers; provides extra mid-bass presence in small trunks.
> 1.00 Underdamped Pronounced +2 dB to +4 dB resonant peak; prolonged transient ringing. Boomy, "one-note" bass. Sputtering recovery on fast double kick pedals.

Calculating the Half-Power Cutoff Frequency ($F_3$)

The frequency where the system's output drops by -3 dB relative to its mid-band sensitivity is calculated using:

$$F_3 = F_c left[ left( rac{1}{2 Q_{tc}^2} - 1 ight) + sqrt{left( rac{1}{2 Q_{tc}^2} - 1 ight)^2 + 1} ight]^{1/2}$$
Notice that when $Q_{tc} = rac{1}{sqrt{2}} approx 0.707$, the term $left( rac{1}{2 Q_{tc}^2} - 1 ight) = 0$, reducing the formula to $F_3 = F_c$.

Thermodynamics: Isothermal vs. Adiabatic Polyfill Damping

When an unlined wooden box compresses air, the process is adiabatic (air heats up as it compresses and cools as it expands, increasing the acoustic stiffness $gamma approx 1.4$).

Lining or loosely stuffing the sealed enclosure with 0.5 to 1.0 pound of polyfill or fiberglass per cubic foot alters the thermodynamics toward an isothermal process ($gamma approx 1.0$). The fine fibers absorb heat from the compressed air, slowing the speed of sound and tricking the subwoofer driver into behaving as if the box volume were 10% to 20% larger. This allows designers to build cabinets up to 15% smaller while maintaining a target 0.707 Butterworth alignment.

Frequently Asked Questions

The acoustic suspension design (invented by Edgar Villchur in 1954) uses a driver with a very compliant mechanical suspension mounted inside a small, airtight enclosure. Instead of relying on a stiff mechanical spider, the trapped pocket of air inside the sealed box acts as a linear pneumatic spring that restores the cone to center, reducing harmonic distortion and providing smooth 12 dB/octave low-frequency roll-off.

Qtc is the total quality factor of the speaker-enclosure system at its resonant frequency ($F_c$). A $Q_{tc}$ of 0.50 is critically damped with zero overshoot and fast transient decay; $Q_{tc}$ of 0.577 (Bessel) provides optimal linear phase and group delay; $Q_{tc}$ of 0.707 (Butterworth) provides the maximally flat passband with the lowest $F_3$ cutoff; and $Q_{tc}$ above 0.9 creates a boomy resonant bass peak suitable for small car audio setups.

A sealed box behaves as a 2nd-order high-pass acoustic filter, resulting in a gentle 12 dB per octave roll-off below its resonant frequency ($F_c$). A ported enclosure is a 4th-order resonant system rolling off at a steep 24 dB per octave. Because vehicle cabin gain and small room boundary gain increase bass at roughly +12 dB per octave, sealed enclosures naturally complement small rooms, often producing usable bass down to 10 Hz.

When air is compressed inside an empty enclosure, it undergoes adiabatic compression where air temperature rises during compression and cools during expansion. Adding loose fibrous damping material (such as Dacron polyfill or acoustic fiberglass) absorbs heat during compression, converting the thermodynamic cycle toward isothermal compression. This slows sound speed and increases apparent enclosure volume by 10% to 20%, lowering $F_c$ and $Q_{tc}$.

If a sealed box is too small, the air spring becomes overly stiff. System resonance ($F_c$) shifts upward and $Q_{tc}$ rises significantly above 0.707, producing an underdamped response with a resonant boom in the 60 Hz to 90 Hz region while sacrificing deep sub-bass extension below 40 Hz. It also reduces transient precision and increases cone ringing.

The volume calculated by the formula ($V_b$) is the net internal acoustic air space. When building the physical wooden box, you must add the volume physically displaced by the subwoofer magnet and basket structure (typically 0.05 to 0.15 cubic feet for a 12-inch driver) plus any internal bracing to determine the gross internal volume.
