A passive crossover calculator gives you the capacitor, inductor, and resistor values for splitting an amplifier's signal between woofer, midrange, and tweeter. Key Takeaways

  • Capacitor (high-pass): C = 1 / (2π × fc × Z) farads. Inductor (low-pass): L = Z / (2π × fc) henries.
  • A 2nd-order (12 dB/octave) crossover shifts phase 180° — reverse the tweeter's polarity or the drivers cancel at fc.
  • Pad a loud tweeter with an L-pad, not a single resistor — only the L-pad keeps impedance (and fc) constant.
  • Calculator values are starting points: driver roll-off, baffle step, and real impedance curves shift final results.
  • Round to standard E12 part values (±5% parts shift fc by about ±5% — usually inaudible).

What Is a Passive Crossover?

Definition: A passive crossover is a filter network of capacitors, inductors, and resistors wired between an amplifier and speaker drivers. It divides the full-range signal into frequency bands — lows to the woofer, mids to the midrange, highs to the tweeter — so each driver only reproduces what it handles cleanly. "Passive" means it needs no power supply, unlike active or DSP crossovers.

No single driver reproduces 20 Hz–20 kHz cleanly: woofers beam and break up at high frequencies, tweeters distort or burn out on bass. The crossover is the traffic cop — a series inductor blocks highs from the woofer, a series capacitor blocks lows from the tweeter, and in 3-way designs a band-pass section (capacitor + inductor) isolates the midrange.

The slope (6, 12, 18, or 24 dB/octave) sets how sharply each band cuts off. Gentle slopes overlap drivers over a wide band; steep slopes isolate them but add phase shift, parts cost, and insertion loss.

Formula + Worked Example

The two master formulas behind every passive crossover:

C = 1 / (2π × fc × Z)  ·  L = Z / (2π × fc)

C in farads (×106 for µF), L in henries (×103 for mH).

Worked example: 2-way crossover at fc = 3,000 Hz, Z = 8 Ω:

  1. Tweeter capacitor: C = 1 / (2π × 3000 × 8) = 1 / 150,796 = 6.63 × 10−6 F = 6.63 µF → buy 6.8 µF (E12).
  2. Woofer inductor: L = 8 / (2π × 3000) = 8 / 18,850 = 4.24 × 10−4 H = 0.42 mH.
  3. For 12 dB/octave Linkwitz-Riley, scale both: 6.63 × 1.414 = 9.38 µF, 0.42 × 1.414 = 0.60 mH per section leg.

L-pad worked example: padding an 8 Ω tweeter by −3 dB. Voltage ratio K = 10(3/20) = 1.4125. Series R1 = 8 × (1.4125 − 1) / 1.4125 = 2.34 Ω; shunt R2 = 8 / (1.4125 − 1) = 19.39 Ω. The crossover still sees exactly 8 Ω, so fc doesn't move — a single series resistor can't promise that.

Inductor Layout: The 90° Rule

Inductors talk to each other magnetically. Two coils mounted side by side couple like a transformer, leaking signal between filter sections and smearing your carefully calculated slopes. The fix is geometric:

  • Mount adjacent inductors with their magnetic axes at 90° to each other (one flat, one on edge).
  • Keep at least 10 cm (4 in) between large coils.
  • Keep inductors away from steel chassis parts and driver magnets.

Air-core coils are worst for crosstalk (their field spreads freely) yet best for sound (no core saturation) — the classic trade-off. If space is tight, orient the most sensitive pair — woofer low-pass and tweeter high-pass inductors — at right angles first.

Baffle Step: The 6 dB Your Calculator Didn't Mention

Below a certain frequency, bass wraps around the cabinet instead of projecting forward — the speaker loses up to 6 dB of low-frequency output. This baffle step kicks in around f ≈ 4560 / baffle-width-in-inches (about 570 Hz for an 8-inch-wide box).

Calculator values ignore it, so a speaker built strictly to the numbers sounds thin. The standard fix is a shelving network in the woofer section restoring 3–6 dB below the step frequency (full 6 dB only for free-standing speakers; near-wall placement needs less). If your finished speaker sounds bright and bass-shy despite "correct" parts, baffle step is the prime suspect.

E12 Rounding: From Math to Parts Bin

You can't buy a 6.63 µF capacitor — parts come in E12 standard values (±10%) or E24 (±5%). Round to the nearest stocked value and check the damage:

Limitations

  • First-order base values. Higher orders need the alignment scaling above — the raw table is a starting point, not a finished LR4 design.
  • Resistive-load assumption. Real driver impedance rises with frequency; without a Zobel network, actual fc and slope drift from the math.
  • Driver acoustic roll-off ignored. A woofer already rolling off acoustically adds its slope to your electrical filter.
  • No baffle-step or diffraction modeling. See the baffle step section — real in-room response needs this compensation.
  • Ideal parts. Inductor DCR (keep under ~0.3 Ω for woofer coils) and capacitor tolerance shift results slightly.

Treat these as design starting values. Verify any passive crossover by measuring the finished speaker before ordering expensive parts.
