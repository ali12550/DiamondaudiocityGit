Formulas & Notes
  • Time Delay (ms): Δt = (D_right - D_left) / 343 × 1000 + Manual Delay
  • Phase Shift (°): Φ = 360 × f × Δt / 1000 where f = crossover frequency in Hz
  • 343 m/s is the approximate speed of sound in air at 20°C.

What Is Speaker Phase Alignment?

A speaker phase alignment calculator tells you how much to delay one speaker so its sound arrives at the same instant as the other — keeping bass full and the stereo image locked. Enter each speaker's distance, your crossover frequency, and any existing delay, and it returns the time offset in milliseconds and the phase shift in degrees. Misaligned speakers partially cancel each other; aligned ones add up.

Definition: Speaker phase alignment is the practice of time-aligning multiple speakers so their wavefronts reach the listening position simultaneously. When arrivals coincide, sound sums constructively (louder, tighter); when they arrive half a cycle apart — 180° out of phase — they cancel, hollowing out bass and smearing imaging.

Key Takeaways

  • Sound travels ~343 m/s — every extra meter of distance costs ~2.9 ms of delay.
  • Phase shift: Φ = 360 × frequency × delay ÷ 1000 — the same delay means more degrees at higher frequencies.
  • Polarity ≠ phase: flipping polarity is a blunt 180° flip at all frequencies; real alignment needs delay.
  • 80 Hz is the THX-recommended crossover — the most common alignment point for subs.
  • Phase wraps: 360° of shift is physically identical to 0° — don't panic at big numbers.

Formula and Worked Example

Δt = Δd ÷ 343 × 1000 [ms]  ·  Φ = 360 × f × Δt ÷ 1000 [°]

Worked example: right speaker is 0.5 m farther than the left; crossover at 80 Hz:

  1. Time delay: 0.5 ÷ 343 × 1000 = 1.46 ms
  2. Phase shift: 360 × 80 × 1.46 ÷ 1000 = 42°
  3. Fix: delay the left (nearer) speaker by 1.46 ms in your DSP — arrivals now coincide.

Same delay, higher frequency: at 500 Hz that 1.46 ms becomes 360 × 500 × 1.46 ÷ 1000 = 263° — nearly cancellation. This is why alignment is judged at the crossover frequency, where both drivers play together.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Left distance: meters from your main seat to the left speaker's driver center.
  2. Right distance: same measurement for the right speaker (or subwoofer vs main).
  3. Crossover frequency: where the two sources overlap — 80 Hz is the standard sub/main point.
  4. Manual delay: any delay already dialed into your processor (usually 0 to start).
  5. Read ms / degrees — then apply that delay to the nearer speaker in your DSP or AVR.

Rule of thumb: ~0.9 ms per foot (2.9 ms per meter) of distance difference. A sub 3 ft farther than the mains needs ~2.6 ms of delay on the mains.

Polarity vs Phase: Stop Confusing Them

The 0°/180° switch on your subwoofer flips polarity — it inverts the signal, a blunt 180° shift at every frequency. Phase is frequency-dependent delay: 1.46 ms is 42° at 80 Hz but 263° at 500 Hz. Polarity can rescue a worst-case 180° cancellation, but it can never fix a 42° or 120° offset — only true time delay does that. If flipping the switch helps a little but bass still sounds off, you need delay, not polarity.

Crossover Slopes and Their Phase Behavior

Your crossover itself adds phase shift between drivers. The two common topologies behave very differently at the crossover point:

CrossoverSlopePhase at crossoverWhat to do
Linkwitz-Riley24 dB/octDrivers in-phase — sums flatThe modern standard; align with delay only
Butterworth12 dB/oct~90° offset between driversCommon in AVRs; small delay tweak finishes the job

This is why two systems with identical distances can need different correction — check what slope your processor or speaker uses before chasing the last few degrees.

Your AV Receiver Already Does This

Modern AV receivers convert the speaker distance settings (in feet or meters) directly into delay — the exact math on this page. Measure carefully during setup: a 1-foot error equals ~0.9 ms, which is 26° at 80 Hz. Auto-calibration systems (Audyssey, YPAO, Dirac) measure it with a microphone, but they can be fooled by ported subwoofers' extra group delay — if auto-setup leaves bass thin, add 1–2 ms to the sub distance manually and listen.

Delay Towers: Phase Alignment at Concert Scale

Outdoor concerts face the same physics at 100× the scale: a delay tower 100 ft from the main PA gets ~89 ms of delay (100 × 0.9 ms/ft) so its wavefront arrives with the mains instead of as an echo. Festival engineers compute it with the same Δt = Δd ÷ 343 formula this calculator uses — just with bigger numbers. Our Sound Distance Drop Calculator covers the level side of the same problem.

Limitations

  • Single-frequency view. The degree readout applies at your crossover frequency; the same delay is a different phase angle at every other frequency.
  • Ignores group delay. Ported subwoofers and steep filters delay bass beyond pure distance — real rooms often need a millisecond or two more than geometry says.
  • One listening position. Perfect alignment at the main seat means slightly-off alignment everywhere else; optimize for where you actually sit.
  • 343 m/s is approximate. Speed of sound shifts with temperature (~0.6 m/s per °C) — irrelevant indoors, worth knowing outdoors.
  • Can't fix reflections. Delay aligns direct arrivals; wall reflections still cause their own comb filtering — see our Room Acoustics Calculator.
