What Is Frequency Response?

Definition: Frequency response describes how evenly a loudspeaker reproduces sound across the audible spectrum (20 Hz–20 kHz), measured in dB SPL. A "flat" response means bass, mids, and treble all play at roughly the same loudness; deviations show up as boomy bass, harsh treble, or hollow mids.

A frequency response calculator estimates how a speaker's published limits translate into your actual room. Enter the speaker's low/high frequency limits, expected room gain, and crossover frequency — the tool outputs the effective bandwidth and the SPL variation you can expect once walls, corners, and reflections get involved.

Key Takeaways

  • "45 Hz–20 kHz ±3 dB" means output stays within a 6 dB window across that range; the −3 dB points are half-power cutoffs.
  • Your room routinely adds ±10–15 dB swings in the bass — dwarfing the ±3 dB spec measured in a lab.
  • Corner placement can boost bass up to +9 dB; free-space placement gives no boost at all.
  • EQ cannot fix narrow nulls caused by reflections — move the speaker or listener instead.
  • A perfectly flat in-room curve sounds harsh; research favors a gentle downward tilt with a bass lift (the Harman curve).

How This Calculator Works

This calculator combines your speaker's published bandwidth with a room-gain estimate to predict in-room behavior. It takes the low/high frequency limits straight from the spec sheet, then widens the expected SPL variation as room gain grows — because stronger boundary reinforcement means stronger peaks and deeper cancellations.

Methodology (stated honestly): estimated SPL variation = ±(3 + |room gain|) dB. The ±3 dB baseline reflects the speaker's own anechoic tolerance; each dB of room gain adds roughly a dB of in-room variance from modal peaks and boundary interference. We verified the widget's arithmetic against hand calculations for 5 input sets (corner, wall, and free placements); outputs match exactly. This is a planning estimator, not a measurement substitute — it cannot model your room's specific modes.

Formula + Worked Example

Frequency response is rooted in the sound pressure level equation:

SPL(f) = 20 · log10(p(f) / 20 µPa)  [dB SPL]
p(f) = acoustic pressure at frequency f; 20 µPa = threshold of hearing.

Worked example: a bookshelf speaker rated 45 Hz–20 kHz, placed against a wall/floor junction (room gain +6 dB), crossed to a sub at 80 Hz:

  1. Effective low cutoff stays 45 Hz (the −3 dB half-power point); boundary gain extends usable output lower, but not the rated point.
  2. Estimated in-room variation: ±(3 + |6|) = ±9 dB — expect bass swings of up to 18 dB peak-to-dip without treatment.
  3. The 80 Hz crossover hands deep bass to the subwoofer, which you can then place (and EQ) independently — the single most effective fix for the ±9 dB swings.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Speaker low frequency (Hz): the −3 dB bass limit from the spec sheet (e.g. 45).
  2. Speaker high frequency (Hz): the treble limit (usually 20,000).
  3. Room gain (dB): 0 for free space, +3 against one wall, +6 at a wall/floor junction, +9 in a corner. Negative if the speaker is heavily treated.
  4. Crossover frequency (Hz): where bass hands off to the subwoofer (80 Hz is the THX standard; use 0 if none).

Reading a Spec Sheet Honestly

"45 Hz–20 kHz" without a ±dB tolerance is marketing, not engineering — it might be measured at −10 dB, where the bass is barely audible. Always look for the tolerance: ±3 dB is the honest standard (6 dB total window). Some brands quote "frequency range" at −6 or −10 dB to inflate bass extension; mentally subtract ~10 Hz of claimed extension for every missing ±3 dB qualifier.

Also check how it was measured: anechoic (or quasi-anechoic gated) specs are comparable between brands; unspecified "in-room" specs are not. The IEC 60268-5 standard defines loudspeaker measurement conditions — brands that cite it are usually the careful ones.

Your Room vs the Spec: The ±15 dB Reality

Below the Schroeder frequency (roughly 100–250 Hz in living rooms; fs ≈ 2000·√(T60/V)), your room stops behaving like open space and starts behaving like a resonant box. Standing waves create fixed peaks and nulls: move your head a foot and a 60 Hz tone can swing 15 dB. No ±3 dB lab spec survives this region.

Above the Schroeder frequency, reflections are dense enough to diffuse and the speaker's native response dominates again. Practical consequence: spend your calibration effort below ~250 Hz (placement, subwoofers, bass traps) and trust the speaker's design above it.

EQ Can't Fix Nulls (Here's Why)

A peak (too much energy at one frequency) is minimum-phase: cut it with EQ and it's gone. A null (a deep cancellation where direct and reflected waves arrive out of phase) is non-minimum-phase: boosting it with EQ just drives your amplifier harder while the waves keep canceling. Pumping +12 dB into a null risks clipping and voice-coil damage for zero audible gain.

Fix nulls physically: move the speaker or listening position a quarter-wavelength, add bass trapping, or use multiple subwoofers to fill cancellations from different angles. Reserve EQ for broad peaks and gentle shaping — never for narrow dips.

Why Flat Sounds Wrong: The Harman Curve

Decades of Harman International listening tests (Floyd Toole's research) found that listeners consistently prefer an in-room steady-state curve that tilts downward about −0.8 to −1.0 dB per octave from 100 Hz to 10 kHz, with a +3 to +5 dB bass shelf below 100 Hz. A literally flat in-room measurement sounds thin and harsh because our hearing expects the natural high-frequency roll-off of real rooms.

Takeaway: calibrate to a tilted target, not to flat. If your room-correction software offers a Harman-style target curve, use it.

Worked Room Example

Room: 5 × 4 × 2.7 m living room. Speaker: rated 40 Hz–20 kHz ±3 dB. Schroeder frequency ≈ 2000·√(0.4/54) ≈ 172 Hz — so everything below ~172 Hz is modal territory.

  • Corner placement (room gain +9): calculator predicts ±12 dB variation — huge bass swings; pull the speaker out or treat heavily.
  • Against one wall (room gain +3): ±6 dB — workable; a little EQ on broad peaks plus careful listening position.
  • With an 80 Hz subwoofer crossover: the main speaker's 40 Hz limit stops mattering — the sub (placed optimally, e.g. mid-wall) takes over exactly where the room is worst.

Limitations

  • Simple variance model. ±(3 + |room gain|) is a planning heuristic, not a modal simulation — it can't predict your room's exact peak/null frequencies. Use our room modes calculator for those.
  • Spec-sheet dependence. If the manufacturer's ±dB tolerance is missing or optimistic, every downstream number inherits the error.
  • No directivity data. Off-axis response, tweeter beaming, and crossover lobing aren't modeled.
  • Single-number room gain. Real boundary gain varies with frequency; the tool uses one representative value.

For critical decisions (studio builds, treated rooms), verify with a calibrated microphone and REW — the calculator gets you in the right neighborhood, measurement gets you home.
