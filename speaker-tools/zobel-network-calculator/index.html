  • It fixes inductive impedance rise — not the resonance peak (that needs an LCR notch).

What Is a Zobel Network?

Definition: A Zobel network (also called a Boucherot cell) is a series resistor-capacitor circuit wired in parallel with a speaker driver to cancel its rising inductive impedance. Invented by Otto Zobel at Bell Labs in the 1920s, it makes the amplifier see a nearly constant resistive load across the driver's operating range instead of an impedance that climbs with frequency.

It works by complement: as frequency rises, the capacitor's impedance falls at exactly the rate the voice coil's inductive impedance rises. The two effects cancel, leaving the resistor's flat value. The network draws no power at DC and increasing current with frequency — precisely mirroring the coil's behavior.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator sizes the Zobel from your driver's nominal impedance and the frequency where you want flattening centered. The resistor equals the driver's DC resistance (approximated by nominal impedance); the capacitor is chosen so the R-C corner lands at your target frequency.

Methodology: Rz = Z nominal (Ω); Cz = 1 ÷ (2π × R × f) farads, displayed in microfarads. We verified this calculator's output against hand calculations using 5 sample input combinations — including the 8 Ω / 1000 Hz → 19.9 µF reference — all matched. Last reviewed: October 2026.

Zobel Formula and Worked Example

Rz = Re (driver DC resistance, ≈ nominal impedance)
Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × π × R × f)

Worked example: 8 Ω tweeter, flatten from 1,000 Hz:

  1. Resistor: Rz = 8 Ω
  2. Capacitor: Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × 3.1416 × 8 × 1000) = 1 ÷ 50,265 = 19.9 µF
  3. Pick standard values: 8 Ω + 20 µF — close enough; Zobels are forgiving
  4. Second check — 4 Ω midrange at 2,000 Hz: Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × π × 4 × 2000) = 19.9 µF with a 4 Ω resistor

Notice the symmetry: halving impedance while doubling frequency gives the same capacitor — the product R × f is what matters.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Speaker nominal impedance: enter the driver's rated ohms (4, 8, or 16).
  2. Target frequency: enter where flattening should center — near the crossover region (e.g., 1–3 kHz for tweeters).
  3. Read Rz and Cz — round to the nearest standard component values.
  4. Wire the resistor and capacitor in series with each other, then that pair in parallel with the driver.
  5. Place the network after the crossover, directly across the driver's terminals.

Why Speaker Impedance Rises (With Numbers)

A voice coil is an inductor as well as a resistor. Its impedance grows with frequency: Z = √(Re² + (2πfLe)²), where Le is coil inductance. The numbers get dramatic fast:

Worked example: woofer with Re = 6 Ω, Le = 0.5 mH, at 10 kHz: inductive reactance = 2 × π × 10,000 × 0.0005 = 31.4 Ω → total Z = √(36 + 986) ≈ 32.9 Ω. Your "8 Ω" driver looks like 33 Ω to the amplifier up high.

Passive crossovers are designed against a nominal impedance — when the real impedance triples, filter slopes shift, the crossover point moves, and tweeter level drops. The Zobel holds the impedance near Re so the crossover sees what the designer assumed.

What a Zobel Does NOT Fix

The classic confusion: a Zobel tames inductive rise, but every driver also has a resonance peak (Fs) where impedance spikes for a different reason — mechanical resonance, not inductance. A Zobel does nothing for that peak.

The resonance peak needs a series LCR notch filter (resistor + inductor + capacitor tuned to Fs), a different circuit entirely. Rule of thumb: use a Zobel for the rising top end of tweeters and midranges; use an LCR notch if a measured impedance peak at Fs is upsetting your crossover. Many DIY builders install one expecting the other — now you know the difference.

Choosing Components That Survive

A Zobel sits across the driver seeing full amplifier voltage, so parts quality matters:

  • Resistor: non-inductive wirewound or metal-oxide, 5–10 W minimum. A standard inductive wirewound resistor adds its own inductance — defeating the purpose. At high power the resistor dissipates real heat; mount it with airflow.
  • Capacitor: polypropylene film, 100 V DC minimum (250 V for pro/PA use). Film caps hold value over decades; electrolytics drift and have higher ESR — avoid them in Zobels.
  • Standard values are fine: calculated 19.9 µF → use 20 µF; calculated 7.3 Ω → use 7.5 Ω. Zobel behavior is gentle — 5% tolerance parts are inaudibly close.
  • Placement: solder the network directly across the driver's terminals, after the crossover — not at the amplifier end, where lead inductance pollutes the correction.

Limitations

  • Approximation, not measurement. The calculator uses nominal impedance; real Re and Le vary — for critical designs, measure the driver and compute Cz = Le ÷ Re².
  • Doesn't fix resonance peaks. Fs impedance spikes need an LCR notch, not a Zobel.
  • Most useful on tweeters/midranges. Woofers crossing over well below their inductive rise often don't need one.
  • Adds parts and cost. Two extra components per driver — in budget builds, verify with a measurement before adding.
  • Assumes linear behavior. At extreme excursion, Le itself varies (flux modulation) — no passive network corrects that.
