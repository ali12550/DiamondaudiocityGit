Bass Reflex Port Length Calculator

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A bass reflex port length calculator tells you how long to cut your vent tube for a target tuning frequency. Enter box volume, tuning frequency, port diameter, and port count — the tool applies the Helmholtz formula with end correction and returns the exact length in centimeters.

Key Takeaways

  • Port length sets the box's Helmholtz tuning frequency (Fb) — the bass boost point.
  • Longer port = lower tuning; wider port or bigger box = shorter port for the same tuning.
  • Formula: L = (23562.5 × D² × N) ÷ (Fb² × Vb) − 0.732 × D (cm).
  • The −0.732 × D term is end correction — air just outside the port counts too.
  • Too-small ports chuff (whistle); keep air velocity modest or add port area and recalculate.

What Is a Bass Reflex Port?

A bass reflex port is a tuned vent in a speaker enclosure that turns the box into a Helmholtz resonator, reinforcing bass output around the tuning frequency (Fb). The air slug in the port bounces on the "spring" of air inside the box — at Fb, the port radiates strongly, extending bass response well below what the sealed box alone could produce.

Get the length wrong and the tuning shifts: too short tunes too high (thin bass, port noise), too long tunes too low (sloppy, inefficient). This calculator nails the length from your box volume and target tuning.

How This Calculator Works

The tool solves the classic vented-box equation for length, given your box volume (Vb, liters), desired tuning (Fb, Hz), port diameter (D, cm), and number of identical ports (N). Multiple ports behave as one larger port, so the formula scales with N.

Methodology: L = (23562.5 × D² × N) ÷ (Fb² × Vb) − 0.732 × D, result in centimeters. The constant 23562.5 folds in the speed of sound and unit conversions; the trailing 0.732 × D is the end correction (see below). Validation method: enter Vb=50, Fb=35, D=7, N=1 → L ≈ 13.7 cm; raising N to 2 substantially increases L for the same tuning.

Last reviewed: October 2026. Formula is the standard cylindrical-vent equation used in loudspeaker design references.

Worked Example: 12" Sub, 50 L Tuned to 35 Hz

A common car-audio build: Vb = 50 L, Fb = 35 Hz, one 7 cm diameter port:

  1. D² × N = 49 × 1 = 49; Fb² × Vb = 1225 × 50 = 61,250
  2. (23562.5 × 49) ÷ 61,250 = 1,154,562.5 ÷ 61,250 ≈ 18.85 cm
  3. End correction: 0.732 × 7 = 5.12 cm
  4. L = 18.85 − 5.12 ≈ 13.7 cm

Want deeper tuning at 28 Hz instead? Fb² drops to 784, so L ≈ (23562.5 × 49 ÷ 39,200) − 5.12 ≈ 24.3 cm — nearly double the length for 7 Hz lower tuning. That steep sensitivity is why precise measurement matters.

How to Use the Port Length Calculator

  1. Box volume (Vb): enter net internal volume in liters (subtract driver, port, and bracing displacement).
  2. Tuning frequency (Fb): enter your target in Hz — 30–35 Hz is typical for music subs.
  3. Port diameter: enter the inside diameter in cm of the tube you'll actually buy.
  4. Number of ports: select 1, 2, or 3 identical ports.
  5. Cut to the result: the physical tube length in cm — measure twice, cut once.

End Correction: Why the Port Is "Longer" Than It Looks

The −0.732 × D term corrects for a subtle physics fact: the air just outside each port end vibrates with the slug inside, so the port behaves acoustically longer than its physical tube. Each end adds roughly 0.366 × D of "virtual" length (×2 ends = 0.732 × D).

Skip the correction and your box tunes higher than intended — a 7 cm port built 5 cm too long in acoustic terms can shift Fb by several Hz. Flared port ends (aeroports) increase the effective correction slightly, which is part of why they also reduce chuffing: smoother airflow at the mouths.

Port Chuffing: When Air Velocity Ruins Bass

Chuffing is the whistling/huffing noise a too-small port makes when air velocity gets turbulent — typically above ~17 m/s peak. It masquerades as distortion and ruins otherwise good bass. The fix is more port area (bigger diameter or more ports), which drops velocity — but changing area shifts tuning, so recalculate length afterward.

Rule of thumb: a single 7–10 cm port suits most 10–12" subs at moderate power; high-excursion monsters or 15"+ drivers want 2 ports or a large slot. If you hear huffing on loud bass notes, the port — not the driver — is the culprit.

Build and Verify: Measure Your Actual Tuning

Don't trust the math blindly — measure Fb after building. The simple method: play sine sweeps near your target tuning while watching the driver cone; at Fb, cone excursion hits a visible minimum (the port does the work). Mark the frequency where movement is smallest — that's your true tuning.

If measured Fb is high, lengthen the port; if low, shorten it. A few cm of adjustment dials in the last few Hz. Also confirm the port has clearance — keep at least one port diameter of free space behind the inner end, or the nearby wall detunes it.

Limitations

  • Cylindrical ports assumed. Slot ports need an equivalent diameter conversion; heavily flared aeros tune slightly lower than calculated.
  • Net volume must be honest. Forgetting to subtract driver/port/bracing displacement is the #1 cause of mistuned boxes.
  • No air-velocity check. The tool gives length, not chuffing risk — size port area sensibly (see above).
  • Driver parameters ignored. True box design starts from Thiele/Small parameters; this tool sizes the vent once you've chosen Vb and Fb.
