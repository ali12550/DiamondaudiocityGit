What Is Sound Reflection and Absorption?

A sound absorption calculator estimates how much sound energy a room's surfaces soak up versus bounce back, and what that means for reverberation time (RT60). It uses surface areas and material absorption coefficients to predict absorbed energy, reflected energy, and approximate decay time.

Definition: Sound absorption is the process by which a material converts sound energy into tiny amounts of heat instead of reflecting it. The absorption coefficient (α) rates this from 0 (total reflection, like concrete) to 1 (total absorption, like an open window), and it changes with frequency.

When sound hits a wall, its energy splits three ways: absorbed (turned to heat in the material), reflected (bounced back into the room), and transmitted (passed through to the other side). For room acoustics we mostly care about the first two, and they trade off directly: a surface that absorbs 30% reflects roughly 70%.

Key Takeaways

  • RT60 = 0.161 × Volume ÷ Absorption (metric) — the Sabine formula this field has used for 125+ years.
  • Absorption coefficients run 0 (mirror-like) to 1 (total absorption); concrete ≈ 0.02, acoustic panels ≈ 0.9.
  • Target RT60: 0.3–0.6 s for home theaters, 1.5–2.2 s for concert halls — there is no universal "best."
  • Thin foam fixes echo, not bass — low frequencies need thick traps in corners.
  • To halve your RT60, you must double the room's total absorption.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator multiplies your surface area by the absorption and reflection coefficients you enter: absorbed energy = area × α, reflected energy = area × reflection coefficient. It then estimates reverberation time with the imperial Sabine formula.

Methodology: RT60 ≈ 0.049 × Room Volume ÷ Total Absorption, using a fixed 1,000 ft³ demo room volume. One "sabin" of absorption = one square foot of perfect absorber, so 200 ft² at α = 0.3 gives 60 sabins → RT60 ≈ 0.049 × 1000 ÷ 60 ≈ 0.82 s. Formula and Worked Example

Wallace Clement Sabine's formula links room volume and absorption to reverberation time:

RT60 = 0.161 × V ÷ A (metric: V in m³, A in m² sabins)

RT60 = 0.049 × V ÷ A (imperial: V in ft³, A in ft² sabins)

Worked example: a 20 × 15 × 10 ft room (3,000 ft³). Surfaces: walls 700 ft² of painted drywall (α 0.05), floor 300 ft² of carpet (α 0.30), ceiling 300 ft² of acoustic tile (α 0.55).

  1. Walls: 700 × 0.05 = 35 sabins
  2. Floor: 300 × 0.30 = 90 sabins
  3. Ceiling: 300 × 0.55 = 165 sabins
  4. Total A = 35 + 90 + 165 = 290 sabins
  5. RT60 = 0.049 × 3000 ÷ 290 = 147 ÷ 290 ≈ 0.51 s — right in the home-theater sweet spot.

Pro Techniques Most Calculator Pages Skip

Sabine vs Eyring: when the classic formula lies

Sabine's formula assumes absorption is spread thinly. In a dead room (average α above ~0.2–0.3), it overestimates RT60 — sometimes badly. The Norris-Eyring formula fixes this: RT60 = 0.161 × V ÷ (−S × ln(1 − ᾱ)).

Worked example: 100 m³ room, 140 m² surfaces, average α = 0.5 → Sabine: A = 70 sabins, RT60 = 0.161 × 100 ÷ 70 ≈ 0.23 s. Eyring: 16.1 ÷ (140 × 0.693) ≈ 0.17 s — Sabine overshoots by ~38%. Rule of thumb: Sabine for lively rooms, Eyring for treated ones.

How many panels do you actually need?

Work backwards from your target: A_needed = 0.161 × V ÷ RT60_target. Example: a 60 m³ room targeting 0.4 s needs 0.161 × 60 ÷ 0.4 ≈ 24 sabins. With α = 0.9 panels, that's 24 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 27 m² of panel (≈ 290 ft²). Subtract absorption you already have (carpet, curtains, furniture) before buying.

NRC ratings, decoded

NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient) is the single number on panel packaging: the average of α at 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz, rounded to 0.05. An NRC 0.85 panel absorbs 85% on average — but the average hides bass performance, where the same panel might manage only 0.3. For home theaters, check the full frequency table, not just NRC.

Treat in this order: reflection points first

Don't randomly cover walls. 1. Find first reflection points with the mirror trick: sit in the listening position while a friend slides a mirror along the side walls — everywhere you see the speaker gets a panel. 2. Put bass traps in corners (low frequencies pile up there). 3. Treat the rear wall — absorption for small rooms, diffusion for larger ones. Plan placement with our Speaker Placement Calculator.

Limitations

  • Single-frequency thinking. The widget uses one α value, but real materials absorb highs far better than lows. A room can measure "treated" yet still boom at 80 Hz.
  • Ignores room modes. Below ~300 Hz, standing waves (room modes) dominate — no Sabine estimate predicts those peaks and nulls.
  • Assumes diffuse field. Sabine works best in reasonably uniform rooms; long corridors and coupled spaces break the model.
  • Fixed demo volume. The widget's RT60 assumes 1,000 ft³ — scale it to your room (see methodology note) instead of taking it literally.
  • No diffusion modeling. Diffusers scatter sound without absorbing it — this tool only counts absorption and reflection.
