    What Is Room Acoustics?

    Definition: Room acoustics is how a room's size, shape, and surfaces shape the sound you hear. Two numbers summarize it: RT60, the seconds for sound to decay 60 dB (reverberation), and the room's modal frequencies, where sound waves fit exactly between walls and pile up into boomy standing waves.

    In small rooms — studios, bedrooms, home theaters — the modes matter more than the reverb. A 15-foot room has its first length mode near 38 Hz; play a bass note there and the room amplifies it, while a note halfway between modes nearly disappears. Move your head a foot and the bass changes completely. That's modal behavior, and no equalizer fully fixes it — only treatment and placement do.

    RT60 describes the statistical wash of reflections above the modal region. Too long and speech smears; too short and the room sounds unnaturally dead. Different uses want different targets (see the table below).

    Formula + Worked Example

    RT60 = 0.161 × V / A  ·  fn = n × c / 2L

    V in m³, A in metric sabins · c = 343 m/s (1125 ft/s) · n = 1, 2, 3…

    Worked example: room 20 × 15 × 9 ft, furnished (α = 0.15):

    1. Volume: 20 × 15 × 9 = 2,700 ft³ = 76.5 m³.
    2. Surface: 2(20×15 + 20×9 + 15×9) = 2(300 + 180 + 135) = 1,230 ft² = 114.3 m².
    3. Absorption: A = 114.3 × 0.15 = 17.1 sabins.
    4. RT60 = 0.161 × 76.5 / 17.1 = 0.72 s — too live for mixing (target 0.2–0.4 s); needs treatment.
    5. First length mode: f1 = 1125 / (2 × 20) = 28.1 Hz; f2 = 56.3 Hz; f3 = 84.4 Hz.

    Schroeder Frequency: Where Modes Stop and Reverb Starts

    Below roughly 2000 × √(RT60 / V) Hz, your room is a collection of individual resonances; above it, reflections blur into statistical reverb. For the worked 76.5 m³ room at 0.72 s RT60: 2000 × √(0.72/76.5) ≈ 194 Hz.

    This dividing line changes your treatment strategy completely. Below it, RT60 tweaks do almost nothing — you need bass traps targeting specific modal frequencies. Above it, absorption panels and diffusion control the decay. Most small-room complaints ("boomy bass", "boxy vocals") live below the Schroeder frequency, which is why foam panels alone never fix them.

    The Mirror Trick for First Reflections

    The most damaging reflections arrive within the first 20 ms — they comb-filter with the direct sound and smear imaging. Find them with the mirror trick: sit in your listening position, have a friend slide a mirror along each side wall, and mark every spot where you see a speaker in the mirror. Those are your first-reflection points.

    Treat those spots with 2–4" broadband absorption. Do the same for the ceiling (a "cloud" panel above the mix position). This single step typically delivers the biggest audible improvement per dollar in any untreated room.

    Flutter Echo: The Clap Test

    Stand in the middle of your room and clap once, sharply. If you hear a metallic ringing tail — like a spring reverb — that's flutter echo: sound bouncing back and forth between parallel bare walls. It murders vocal clarity and stereo imaging.

    Fix it by breaking the parallelism's symmetry: absorption on one of the two walls is enough (both is overkill), or add diffusion to scatter the reflections in time. Bookshelves full of uneven books are a surprisingly effective folk remedy — genuine diffusion, not absorption.

    Room Ratios: Don't Build a Cube

    When two room dimensions match — or one is a simple multiple of another — their modes stack at the same frequency and boom twice as hard. A 12×12×8 ft room is an acoustic disaster: length and width modes coincide at every harmonic.

    Favored ratios spread modes evenly (Bolt's criteria). Classics include 1 : 1.26 : 1.59 and 1 : 1.6 : 2.33 (height : width : length). If you're stuck with bad ratios, the cure is the same as always: more bass trapping at the stacked frequencies, and never place your listening seat at a null.

    Bass Traps: Why Corners, How Deep

    Bass pressure is highest where room boundaries meet — corners, especially tri-corners where two walls meet the ceiling or floor. That's where traps absorb the most modal energy per square foot. A trap on a flat wall mid-room does a fraction of the work.

    Depth is everything: porous absorption only works where air moves, and bass wavelengths are huge. Effective bass traps start at 4 inches deep (straddling corners to gain depth) and the classic "superchunk" — a floor-to-ceiling triangle of insulation stuffed into the corner — remains the price/performance king. The 1-inch foam squares sold as "bass traps" are not bass traps.

    Limitations

    • Axial modes only. Tangential and oblique modes exist too but are weaker; the widget lists the dominant three per dimension.
    • Single-number RT60. Real RT60 varies with frequency — bass decays longer. The widget gives a mid-band estimate.
    • Absorption presets are averages. Your actual α depends on furnishings; measure with a clap and an RTA app for ground truth.
    • Rectangular rooms assumed. L-shaped rooms, vaulted ceilings, and open floor plans break the simple models.
    • No speaker/listener positions. Mode excitation depends on where the sub and your ears sit relative to nodes — the frequencies are fixed, the audibility isn't.
