How This Converter Works

In audio engineering and modern music production, time-based effects (delays, reverbs, flangers, tremolos, and compressors) sound significantly cleaner and more cohesive when their timing parameters are locked to the tempo of the song.

This tool operates in two interchangeable directions:

The Mathematical Formulas

One minute contains exactly 60 seconds, which equals $60{,}000\text{ milliseconds}$. Because musical meter defines tempo as beats per minute, the duration of one quarter-note beat ($1/4\text{ note}$) in standard 4/4 time is:

Quarter Note ($1/4$) Quarter Note (ms) = 60,000 ÷ BPM The fundamental timing pulse of 1 beat
Any Note Value ms = (60,000 ÷ BPM) × Note Beats Multiply quarter note by beat multiplier
Dotted Note (.) Dotted ms = Normal ms × 1.5 Extends the note duration by half its value
Triplet Note (T) Triplet ms = Normal ms × (2 ÷ 3) Compresses 3 notes into the time of 2

Practical Mixing & Sound Design Applications

1. The Iconic Dotted 8th-Note Delay Effect

The dotted 8th note ($375.00\text{ ms}$ at 120 BPM or $351.56\text{ ms}$ at 128 BPM) is one of the most widely used delay timings in contemporary music. When played over straight quarter notes, it creates a syncopated, galloping counter-rhythm. Famous examples include the signature guitar tones of U2's The Edge ("Where the Streets Have No Name"), Pink Floyd's "Run Like Hell", and modern trance and synthwave arpeggios.

2. Calculating Reverb Pre-Delay

Pre-delay dictates the gap in time between the dry sound (such as a lead vocal or snare drum) and the initial onset of the reverberant space. If pre-delay is set to 0 ms, the reverb immediately washes over the vocal, pushing it backward in the mix and causing intelligibility loss. By setting pre-delay to a 1/32 note ($62.5\text{ ms}$ at 120 BPM) or a 1/64 note ($31.25\text{ ms}$), the lead vocal hits upfront and clean before the lush reverb envelope blooms behind it.

3. Musical Reverb Decay Time ($RT_{60}$)

While reverb decay time ($RT_{60}$) depends on room size and aesthetic preference, setting total decay to a multiple of musical bars (such as 1 bar = $2.00\text{ s}$ or 2 bars = $4.00\text{ s}$ at 120 BPM) prevents reverb tails from overlapping chaotically into the next downbeat or chord change.

4. Triplet Delays for Dub Techno, Reggae & Psytrance

Triplet delays (such as a 1/8T at $166.67\text{ ms}$ or 1/16T at $83.33\text{ ms}$ at 120 BPM) introduce a swinging, 3-against-2 polyrhythm that fits perfectly into shuffle beats, reggae tape dubs, and driving psytrance basslines without cluttering the downbeats.

5. Slapback Echo for Vintage Vocals & Guitars

Classic 1950s rockabilly and John Lennon slapback delay relies on a single echo repetition set between 75 ms and 130 ms. At 120 BPM, a straight 1/16 note ($125\text{ ms}$) provides an authentic, tempo-locked slapback that thickens a lead vocal without sounding like an echo chamber.

6. Synthesizer LFO Modulation Rates in Hertz (Hz)

Hardware synthesizers and analog modular Eurorack gear frequently express LFO speed in Hertz (cycles per second) rather than rhythmic note divisions. Use our lower frequency readout to set your LFO rate:

  • 0.500 Hz: Exactly 1 full LFO cycle across 1 measure (4 beats at 120 BPM). Ideal for sweeping filter sweeps across an arrangement.
  • 2.000 Hz: Exactly 1 cycle per quarter note. Ideal for rhythmic sidechain pumping or tremolo.
  • 4.000 Hz: Exactly 1 cycle per eighth note. Ideal for rotary speaker chorus and pulsing synth pads.

Common Mistakes When Syncing Delays

  • Forgetting to Filter Delay Tails: Even perfectly timed delays will muddy your mix if low frequencies collide with your bass. Apply a high-pass filter (around 100–150 Hz) and low-pass filter (around 4–6 kHz) directly on your delay return auxiliary bus.
  • Neglecting Stereo Ping-Pong Offsets: When setting up dual stereo delays, setting the Left channel to a straight 1/8 note ($250\text{ ms}$) and the Right channel to a dotted 1/8 note ($375\text{ ms}$) creates incredible stereo width without phase cancellation.
  • Confusing Pre-Delay with Decay: Pre-delay is the initial wait time before reflections begin; decay time ($RT_{60}$) is how long those reflections take to fade into silence. Both can and should be musically synced using this calculator.
