What Is BPM to MS Conversion?

Definition: BPM to ms conversion translates musical tempo (beats per minute) into millisecond time durations for each note value. A quarter note at 120 BPM lasts exactly 500 ms; every other subdivision — eighths, triplets, dotted notes — is a fixed fraction or multiple of that value. Producers use these numbers to sync delays, reverbs, and modulation to the track's tempo.

Every tempo-synced effect in your DAW is secretly doing this math. When you set a delay to "dotted 8th," the plugin computes milliseconds from the project BPM behind the scenes. Knowing the numbers yourself matters whenever sync isn't available: hardware pedals, vintage-style plugins without sync, reverb pre-delays (which most reverbs only accept in ms), and DJ effects on standalone gear.

How This BPM to MS Calculator Works

This calculator works in two directions. BPM → ms mode takes a tempo and returns the millisecond duration of every standard note subdivision — whole, half, quarter, eighth, sixteenth, and 32nd notes, each in straight, dotted, and triplet forms — plus the LFO rate in Hz. ms → BPM mode reverses the math: enter a millisecond value (a loop length, a delay time, a sample) and get the matching tempo.

Methodology: quarter note ms = 60,000 ÷ BPM; subdivisions multiply by fixed ratios (eighth = ×0.5, dotted eighth = ×0.75, eighth triplet = ×0.333); BPM = 60,000 ÷ ms for reverse mode; LFO Hz = BPM ÷ 60 per quarter note. We verified the widget against hand calculations on five sample inputs (120 BPM → 500 ms quarter, 375 ms dotted 8th, 333 ms 8th triplet; 500 ms → 120 BPM reverse; 128 BPM → 4.27 Hz eighth-note LFO) — all matched exactly.

BPM to MS Formulas and Worked Example

The number 60,000 is simply milliseconds per minute. One beat at 120 BPM takes half a second — 500 ms — and everything else follows by ratio:

  • Quarter note = 60,000 ÷ BPM
  • Eighth note = 30,000 ÷ BPM (half a quarter)
  • Dotted eighth = 45,000 ÷ BPM (quarter × 0.75)
  • Eighth-note triplet = 20,000 ÷ BPM (quarter × 1/3)
  • Sixteenth = 15,000 ÷ BPM · 32nd = 7,500 ÷ BPM
  • Dotted anything = straight value × 1.5 · Triplet anything = straight value × 2/3
  • ms → BPM = 60,000 ÷ ms · LFO Hz = BPM ÷ 60

Worked example: dotted-eighth delay at 128 BPM:

  1. Quarter note: 60,000 ÷ 128 = 468.75 ms
  2. Dotted eighth = quarter × 0.75: 468.75 × 0.75 = 351.6 ms ≈ 352 ms
  3. Check the triplet for contrast: 468.75 × 1/3 = 156.3 ms per eighth-note triplet
  4. LFO at eighth notes: (128 ÷ 60) × 2 = 4.27 Hz

Why 60,000? The Number Behind Every Value

There are 60,000 milliseconds in one minute. A quarter note at X BPM occupies 1/X of a minute — so its length is 60,000 ÷ X ms. That's the entire secret: every subdivision is just a fraction of a minute. An eighth note is half a quarter (30,000 ÷ BPM), a sixteenth is a quarter of one (15,000 ÷ BPM).

Dotted and triplet values need no memorization either. A dot adds half the note's value (×1.5), so a dotted eighth is three sixteenths. A triplet squeezes three notes into the space of two (×2/3), so an eighth-note triplet is one third of a quarter. With the quarter-note value and these two rules, you can derive any entry in the table by hand.

Pre-Delay: The Reverb Setting Everyone Ignores

Pre-delay is the gap in milliseconds between the dry signal and the start of the reverb tail. Set it to a tempo-synced subdivision — typically a 32nd or 64th note (about 15–60 ms at common tempos) — and the reverb breathes with the track instead of smearing the transient. Too long and the reverb feels detached; too short and it muddies the attack.

Most reverb plugins only accept pre-delay in ms, not note values, which is exactly why this calculator earns its place: look up the 64th-note value at your BPM and dial it in. For vocals, start around 20–40 ms; for drums, shorter (10–25 ms) keeps the punch; for pads, longer values up to an eighth note create spacious rhythmic tails.

Reverse Mode: Finding the Tempo of Loops and Samples

Got a drum loop with no tempo tag? Measure its length in milliseconds and reverse the formula. A 2-bar loop at 120 BPM lasts 4,000 ms; feed 4,000 ms into ms → BPM mode and you get 120 BPM back. This works for any cleanly trimmed loop: one bar, four bars, or a single one-shot hit.

The catch is trim accuracy: a loop cut 50 ms too long reads several BPM slow. Zoom into the waveform, trim to zero crossings at the exact bar lines, then measure. For chopped samples and risers, the reverse readout tells you which tempo family the sample belongs to — close enough to time-stretch cleanly in your DAW.

Why Your "Synced" Delay Still Sounds Off

Four culprits, in order of likelihood. Wrong subdivision: you wanted dotted-eighth groove but set straight eighths — half the rhythmic feel lives in that dot. Swing: on shuffled or swung tracks, mathematically perfect straight delays fight the groove; nudge the ms value a few percent late or use your DAW's swing-matched delay. DAW sync vs. manual ms: hardware pedals and some plugins don't follow tempo changes mid-song — if the track ramps tempo, only true sync tracks it. Sample rounding: at 44.1 kHz, one sample is 0.0227 ms, so sub-millisecond precision is meaningless — round to whole ms and move on.

LFO Rates in Hz From Any BPM

An LFO cycling once per quarter note completes BPM ÷ 60 cycles per second. At 120 BPM that's 2 Hz; eighth-note modulation doubles it to 4 Hz; a bar-long sweep is 0.5 Hz. Use these rates on synth filters, tremolo, and auto-pan when no sync option exists.

The musical ones to memorize: eighth-note wobble = BPM ÷ 30 Hz, quarter-note pulse = BPM ÷ 60 Hz, bar-long sweep = BPM ÷ 240 Hz (in 4/4). Dubstep's classic wobble at 140 BPM on eighth notes: 140 ÷ 30 ≈ 4.67 Hz.

Limitations

  • Assumes straight time. Swing, shuffle, and rubato need manual adjustment — math can't feel groove.
  • Millisecond rounding. Values are rounded to whole or tenth ms; at 44.1/48 kHz sample rates, sub-millisecond differences don't exist in practice.
  • One tempo at a time. Songs with tempo ramps or meter changes need per-section calculations.
  • Reverse mode needs clean loops. Sloppy trims and reverb tails inflate the ms reading and deflate the BPM.
  • LFO Hz is quarter-note based. For dotted or triplet modulation, multiply by 1.5 or 2/3 respectively.
