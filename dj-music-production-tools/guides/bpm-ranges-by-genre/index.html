BPM Ranges by Genre: Typical Tempo Chart

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

Dance music genres cluster around signature tempos: house sits near 120–130 BPM, techno at 125–145, dubstep at 138–145, and drum and bass at 170–176. These BPM ranges are conventions, not rules — they grew out of dance floors, equipment limits, and production habits. This guide charts typical tempos across 25+ genres, explains why ranges overlap and drift, and shows how DJs use them to plan mixes, energy, and cross-genre transitions.

Key Takeaways

  • Typical ranges: house 115–130, techno 125–145, trance 128–140, dubstep 138–145, drum and bass 170–176, hip-hop 80–100, reggaeton 85–100 BPM.
  • BPM ranges are guidelines. Streaming catalogs show real songs routinely landing far outside the "official" ranges.
  • Half-time drum patterns make 140 BPM dubstep feel like 70 BPM — and DJ software often reports the half-time number by mistake.
  • Overlap is normal: house and trance both live near 128–130 BPM. BPM alone never identifies a genre.
  • DJs build sets as BPM arcs (warm-up → peak → close) and use the pitch fader — ±8% is about ±10 BPM at 128 — to bridge adjacent tempos.
  • New to the term itself? Start with what BPM means in music.

Why Genres Cluster at Certain Tempos

Genre tempos are not random — they converge for three practical reasons: bodies, equipment, and momentum.

Bodies come first. Dancing has a comfortable cadence, and music follows it. Research on musical preference — including a 2019 study in Frontiers in Psychology — found that preferred tempo sits around 120–130 BPM across cultures. That may be why so many dance genres independently converged on the 120s: it is fast enough to energize a room and sustainable enough to dance to for hours. Genres built for listening rather than dancing (hip-hop, R&B, reggaeton) settled lower, where lyrics and groove have room to breathe.

Equipment set the lanes. Vinyl turntables like the Technics SL-1200 offered a ±8% pitch range, so tracks had to sit within about ±10 BPM of each other to mix. Producers aiming for club play naturally wrote inside the mixable window of neighboring genres, and the whole ladder in the chart above reflects that constraint.

Momentum keeps them there. Once a genre's classics establish a tempo, new producers copy reference tracks, sample packs ship at that tempo, and DJ pools sort by it. The range becomes self-reinforcing — until a new scene deliberately breaks it, the way dubstep slowed UK garage's 130s down to a half-time 140.

Half-Time Feels: Why 140 Sounds Like 70

Dubstep is produced at 138–145 BPM, yet dancers and listeners describe it as slow. The trick is in the drums: the snare lands every two beats instead of every beat, so your body locks onto a pulse at half the notated tempo.

The math is simple. At 140 BPM, each quarter-note beat lasts 60 ÷ 140 = 0.43 seconds. A half-time snare pattern puts the felt pulse at 70 BPM, where each beat lasts 60 ÷ 70 = 0.86 seconds — twice as long. Your ears hear the 140 hi-hats; your body follows the 70 snare.

This is also why DJ software constantly misreads these genres. Detection algorithms often lock onto the half-time drum pattern and report 70 BPM for a 140 BPM dubstep track (or 87 for a 174 drum and bass track counted at half). When beatmatching, use the notated tempo — the fader math only works against the grid your software is actually playing. How DJs Use BPM Ranges in a Set

DJs use genre tempos the way chefs use a recipe: as a plan they can improvise around. The standard plan is an energy arc — open around 115–122 BPM while the floor fills, climb to a 126–132 peak, then ease back toward 118–124 for the close. Genre ranges tell the DJ which records can carry each phase of that arc.

The pitch fader does the bridging. A ±8% fader at 128 BPM covers 128 × 0.92 = 117.8 up to 128 × 1.08 = 138.2 — about ±10 BPM. That means a 124 BPM deep house track can reach a 132 BPM techno record (+6.5%, well within range), but a 120 BPM house track cannot reach 145 BPM hard techno. For big jumps, DJs use transition records at middle tempos, mix during breakdowns where the beat drops out, or switch the deck to a wide tempo range (±16% or more) with key lock engaged. Ranges also warn against lazy mixing. Two genres can share a BPM and still clash — 128 BPM big-room house and 128 BPM trance have different drum weights, bass designs, and breakdown structures, so the transition needs phrasing and EQ work, not just matched numbers. Subgenre Zoom: Where the Families Split

Within each genre family, subgenres carve out narrower tempo lanes. Knowing them helps producers pick project tempos and helps DJs sort crates:

  • House family: nu disco (110–122) → deep house (115–125) → afro house (118–125) → tech house (122–130) → big room (126–130). Tempo rises with intensity.
  • Techno family: dub techno (118–128) → melodic techno (122–130) → peak-time techno (132–145) → hard techno (145–160). The harder the kick, the faster the grid.
  • Bass family: dubstep (138–145, half-time feel) → trap (140–150, half-time feel) → hardstyle (145–155) → footwork (150–165) → hardcore (160–200). Speed and aggression scale together here.
  • Breakbeat family: UK garage (130–135) → jungle (155–170) → drum and bass (170–176). Broken rhythms run faster than four-on-the-floor at the same energy level — the syncopation needs the extra speed to hit.

Common Mistakes With Genre BPM Ranges

  • Treating ranges as rules. A 131 BPM track in a "128 genre" is not wrong — charts describe centers, not fences. Judge the track, not the number.
  • Trusting auto-detected BPM in half-time genres. Software reads 70 for 140 BPM dubstep and trap constantly. If a reading looks halved, double it before beatmatching.
  • Assuming overlap means interchangeability. 128 BPM house and 128 BPM trance mix at the same speed but carry different energy, drums, and breakdowns. Match the vibe, not just the number.
  • Pitching too far without key lock. Beyond about ±4%, vocals and melodic elements start sounding noticeably off. Big tempo jumps need key lock or a planned transition.
  • Playing a whole set at one BPM. A flat 128 all night fatigues the floor. The arc — up and back down — is what makes peak moments feel like peaks.
