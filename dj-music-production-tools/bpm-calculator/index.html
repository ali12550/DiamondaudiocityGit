What Is BPM?

Definition: BPM (beats per minute) is the standard unit of musical tempo, counting how many quarter-note beats occur in one minute. A track at 120 BPM plays two beats per second; at 60 BPM it plays one beat per second. Every DAW, DJ deck, and metronome uses BPM as its common tempo language.

Tempo is one of the three variables this calculator juggles, alongside elapsed time and beat count. They lock together mathematically: fix any two and the third is determined. That relationship is what makes the calculator useful in both directions — finding the tempo of a recorded loop, or finding how long your 64-bar arrangement will run at a chosen tempo.

BPM Formulas and Worked Example

All three modes derive from one relationship: beats per minute means beats divided by minutes.

  • BPM = (Number of beats ÷ Seconds) × 60
  • Seconds = (Number of beats ÷ BPM) × 60
  • Beats = (BPM ÷ 60) × Seconds
  • Beat duration = 60,000 ÷ BPM (milliseconds)

Worked example: you count 32 beats in 16 seconds of a loop:

  1. Divide beats by seconds: 32 ÷ 16 = 2 beats per second
  2. Multiply by 60: 2 × 60 = 120 BPM
  3. Beat duration: 60,000 ÷ 120 = 500 ms per beat
  4. One 4/4 bar (4 beats): 500 × 4 = 2,000 ms = 2 seconds

Reverse check with Calculate Time mode: a 32-beat section at 120 BPM lasts (32 ÷ 120) × 60 = 16 seconds — consistent both ways.

Beatmatching Math: Syncing Two Tracks

To beatmatch, express the tempo gap as a pitch percentage: pitch % = (target BPM − current BPM) ÷ current BPM × 100. Move the slower track's pitch fader up by that amount and the tempos lock.

Worked example: mixing a 122 BPM track into a 126 BPM track: (126 − 122) ÷ 122 × 100 = +3.28%. Push the 122 track's pitch fader to +3.3% and both run at 126. Standard DJ pitch faders cover ±8%, which handles gaps up to about 10 BPM at house tempos — wider gaps need the BPM to Speed Calculator or a planned transition track. The Time-Signature Trap

This calculator counts quarter-note beats in 4/4 time — the default of virtually all electronic music. Two common situations break that assumption. In 6/8 time (common in ballads and Afro-Cuban music), the beat is a dotted quarter, so counting all eighth notes doubles your reading. In half-time / double-time feels (trap at "140" that feels like 70, drum & bass counted at 87), the same audio gives two valid BPM numbers depending on which pulse you count.

The fix is awareness, not math: decide which pulse the drums emphasize, count that, and note the feel. If your result says 70 BPM on a track that clearly bangs like 140, you counted the half-time pulse — double it.

Limitations

  • Assumes steady tempo. Live drummers drift; rubato passages breathe. The result is an average over your measured interval — count longer sections (32+ beats) for a stable average.
  • Assumes 4/4 quarter-note beats. 6/8, 3/4, and half-time feels need manual interpretation (see above).
  • Garbage in, garbage out. A one-beat miscount over 16 seconds shifts the result by nearly 4 BPM at 120. Count carefully or measure twice.
  • Genre match is approximate. Tempo hints at genre but doesn't define it — 128 BPM could be house, trance, or pop.
