    What Is Mix Compatibility?

    Mix compatibility is a measure of whether two tracks can be blended smoothly in a DJ set. It judges two things: do the musical keys harmonize (so melodies and basslines don't clash), and are the tempos close enough to beatmatch without audible pitch damage? When both pass, a transition sounds like one continuous piece of music.

    DJs call the key side of this harmonic mixing — choosing the next track by musical key instead of by feel alone. The tempo side is beatmatching: aligning the beats per minute so both tracks run in sync. Most train-wreck transitions fail on one of these two axes, which is why this checker tests both at once. If you don't know a track's key yet, run it through our song key finder first.

    Compatibility Score Formula and Worked Example

    The overall formula is simple addition:

    Total score = Key score (−1 to +3) + BPM score (−1 to +3)

    Worked example: Track A is 128 BPM in 8A; Track B is 126 BPM in 9A.

    1. Key score: 8A → 9A is +1 step, same letter = Adjacent Key = +2.
    2. BPM score: |128 − 126| = 2 BPM difference = Easy Mix = +2.
    3. Total: 2 + 2 = 4 → Good Match. The energy rises a fifth while the tempo nudge stays inside the easy zone.

    Contrast that with 8A at 128 BPM against 3B at 128 BPM: the tempo is a perfect +3, but the keys clash (−1), giving a total of 2 — only a Possible Mix. This is exactly why tempo alone never guarantees a good blend.

    Pro Mixing Techniques

    Energy boost mixing: raising intensity a perfect fifth

    Moving +1 Camelot number (e.g. 8A → 9A) shifts the music up a perfect fifth — 7 semitones. Our ears hear a fifth as a lift: the same melody feels brighter and more urgent. DJs use this "energy boost" to escalate a set before a peak-time track. Moving −1 does the reverse, cooling the room down. Try the shift with our key transposition tool to hear the interval first.

    Why ±3 BPM is the "easy" line

    The widget calls a 1–3 BPM gap an Easy Mix because of pitch-fader math. A standard DJ pitch fader spans ±8% (Pioneer CDJ). At 128 BPM, 3 BPM is only a 2.3% shift — comfortably inside the fader, with no audible strain. A 10+ BPM gap needs ~8% or more, which sits at the fader's edge and is where most DJs engage key lock or choose another track.

    Key lock: when it saves a mix and when it wrecks it

    Key lock (Master Tempo) holds a track's musical pitch steady while you change its tempo, using time-stretching. It saves mixes with 6–10 BPM gaps — but past roughly ±6% shift, time-stretching smears transients and thins vocals, an artifact dancers can hear. Rule of thumb: inside ±3 BPM, leave key lock off; from ±4 to ±8 BPM, switch it on; past that, pick a closer track.

    Half-time and double-time transitions

    Tracks at a 0.5× or 2× tempo ratio — say 70 BPM against 140 BPM — share the same beat grid: every downbeat of the slower track lands on the faster one's. That makes them mixable despite a huge BPM number gap (the widget will flag it Difficult, and it's right to warn you). Hip-hop DJs ride 70↔140 transitions constantly; the trick is matching phrases, not just beats, so the switch lands on a natural break.

    Camelot vs Open Key Notation

    Camelot (1A–12B, from Mixed In Key) and Open Key (e.g. 1m–12m / 1d–12d, used by Traktor) describe the same 24 keys with different labels. Use whichever your DJ software displays — the mixing rules are identical.

    CamelotOpen KeyMusical key
    8A6mA minor
    8B6dC major
    9A7mE minor
    9B7dG major
    11A9mG minor
    11B9dB♭ major

    Converting is mechanical: Open Key's number runs opposite to Camelot's, and "m" = minor (A side), "d" = major (B side). Rekordbox and Serato show Camelot-style codes; Traktor shows Open Key — check which one your library uses before a gig.

    Limitations

    • Key detection isn't perfect. Software key analysis mislabels roughly 1 in 10 tracks (genre-dependent); if a "Perfect Match" sounds wrong, trust your ears and re-analyze.
    • Energy and phrasing aren't scored. Two compatible tracks can still clash if one has a vocal over the other's drop — phrase matching matters as much as key.
    • Genre conventions differ. Hip-hop and drum & bass DJs routinely break harmonic rules with cuts and doubles; the rubric fits smooth house/techno-style blending best.
    • Live key changes fool the math. Tracks that modulate mid-song (common in pop) get one label but contain two keys.

    This tool is for informational and practice purposes. Key and tempo analysis is an estimate — always confirm by ear before a live set.
