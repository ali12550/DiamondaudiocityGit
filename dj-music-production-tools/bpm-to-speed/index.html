A BPM to speed calculator converts a tempo change into a playback speed multiplier and pitch percentage. Moving a 100 BPM track to 120 BPM needs 1.20× speed — a +20% pitch shift. DJs, producers, and video editors use it to match tempos, transpose keys, and retime media precisely.

Key Takeaways

  • Speed multiplier = target BPM ÷ original BPM — 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20×.
  • Pitch % = (target − original) ÷ original × 100 — the same change as +20%.
  • +1 semitone = ×1.05946 speed (+5.95%) — the number behind every key change.
  • Duration scales inversely: new length = old length ÷ multiplier.
  • Key lock holds pitch while changing tempo, but adds artifacts past roughly ±10%.

What Is BPM to Speed Conversion?

Definition: BPM to speed conversion expresses a tempo change as a playback speed multiplier. Raising a track from 100 to 120 BPM means playing it 1.20× faster — a +20% pitch increase. The multiplier, the percentage, and the semitone shift are three views of the same change.

Speed and pitch are locked together in traditional playback: spin a record faster and it gets higher as well as quicker. Digital tools can separate them with key lock (also called Master Tempo), but the underlying math never changes. Whether you're beatmatching on CDJs, warping a sample in a DAW, or speeding up a podcast, you're applying the same ratio.

BPM to Speed Formulas and Worked Example

Three formulas cover every conversion you'll ever need:

  • Speed multiplier = Target BPM ÷ Original BPM
  • Pitch change % = (Target − Original) ÷ Original × 100
  • Semitones = 12 × log₂(Speed multiplier) — cents = semitones × 100

Worked example: pitching a 100 BPM track up to 120 BPM:

  1. Multiplier: 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20×
  2. Pitch change: (120 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = +20%
  3. Semitones: 12 × log₂(1.20) = 12 × 0.263 = +3.16 semitones (about a minor third)
  4. Duration effect: a 4-minute track now runs 4 ÷ 1.20 = 3 minutes 20 seconds

The Three Numbers, Disentangled

Multiplier, speed %, and pitch % describe the identical change — they just start counting from different places. Speed % = multiplier × 100, and pitch % = speed % − 100. So 1.20× = 120% speed = +20% pitch. Confusion usually comes from hardware: Pioneer-style faders show pitch % (±8%), while DAW warp dialogs and video players show speed multiplier or % (1.00×). Convert between them by adding or subtracting 100.

The semitone readout is the musician's view: it tells you how far the key moves, which matters for harmonic mixing. A +20% pitch shift is +3.16 semitones — enough to clash with the outgoing track unless key lock is on or the keys are compatible.

The 5.946% Rule: Semitones From Speed

One semitone equals a speed ratio of 21/12 = 1.05946 — a +5.946% pitch change. Memorize that number and you can estimate any key shift in your head:

Key Lock On or Off? The Quality Tradeoff

Key lock (Master Tempo on Pioneer, Keylock on Denon) uses time-stretching to change tempo while holding pitch constant. It's essential for harmonic mixing across wide BPM gaps — but it's not free. Time-stretch algorithms smear transients, and the artifacts grow audible past roughly ±10%, especially on vocals, acoustic drums, and already-compressed audio.

Practical policy: key lock ON for harmonic blends wider than ±6%, where key clash is the bigger risk; key lock OFF for small adjustments (±3% or less), scratching, and turntablism, where the natural pitch nudge sounds better and the algorithm would only add smear. If a track sounds phasey or "underwater" with key lock on, that's the algorithm — either narrow the gap or switch it off.

Speed Math for Video Editors

The same ratios retime video. Converting 24 fps film to 25 fps PAL is a 25÷24 = 1.0417× speed-up (+4.17% pitch — the reason PAL audio sounds slightly sharp). Slow motion at 50% speed doubles duration and drops pitch an octave. Learning playback at 0.75× stretches a 4-minute lesson to 5:20 while dropping pitch about 5 semitones — fine for watching, useless for playing along unless your player has pitch-preserving slowdown.

Limitations

  • Assumes linear resampling. The math describes simple speed change; key-lock time-stretch follows the same ratios but adds algorithm artifacts the numbers can't show.
  • Extreme shifts degrade audio. Beyond about ±25%, even good time-stretch sounds unnatural — plan transitions instead of forcing them.
  • Semitone math is equal-temperament. Real instruments drift from perfect 12-TET; a calculated +3.00 semitones may beat slightly against a live-recorded track.
  • Doesn't model swing or groove. Ratios are exact; feel is not — a mathematically perfect match can still sound off on swung material.
