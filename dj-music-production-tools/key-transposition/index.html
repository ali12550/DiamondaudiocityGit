Key Transposition Calculator: Transpose Any Key

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A key transposition calculator shifts any musical key, chord, or melody up or down by semitones — instantly showing the new key, the interval name, guitar capo positions, and concert-pitch equivalents for transposing instruments. Pick a key, choose an interval, done.

Key Takeaways

  • Transposition = same music, new key. Every note moves by the same semitone interval; relationships stay identical.
  • 12 semitones = 1 octave. Common moves: +2 (whole step), +5 (perfect fourth), +7 (perfect fifth).
  • Capo trick: capo at fret X = play shapes X semitones lower to stay in the original key.
  • B♭ instruments (trumpet, clarinet) sound 2 semitones below written; E♭ alto sax sounds 9 below.
  • Spell the new key correctly: sharps going up, flats going down (mostly).

What Is Key Transposition?

Definition: Key transposition is the process of shifting a piece of music from one key to another by moving every note up or down by the same number of semitones. The melody, chords, and intervals keep their exact relationships — only the pitch level changes. Transposing a song from G major up 2 semitones gives A major, with every chord shifted identically.

Musicians transpose constantly: singers need songs in comfortable vocal ranges, guitarists use capos to keep open-string voicings, and arrangers rewrite parts for transposing instruments like trumpets and saxophones. DJs meet the same idea as pitch shifting — a +5.95% speed change is a one-semitone transposition (see the BPM to Speed Calculator).

How This Transposition Calculator Works

Select your original key root and scale mode (major or minor), then enter the semitone shift — positive for up, negative for down — or tap a quick interval button (perfect fourth, perfect fifth, octave). The calculator returns the new key with correct enharmonic spelling, the interval name (e.g. +7 = perfect fifth), the guitar capo fret for the move, and concert-pitch equivalents for B♭, E♭, and F instruments.

Methodology: chromatic index arithmetic mod 12 on the 12-tone equal-temperament circle (C=0 … B=11); enharmonic spelling prefers sharps for upward and flats for downward motion; capo fret = semitones mod 12; transposing-instrument offsets applied from concert pitch. We verified the widget against hand calculations on five sample inputs (G +5 → C; C −4 → A♭; D +7 → A; F♯ −2 → E; B♭ +2 concert for trumpet → C written) — every result matched exactly.

Transposition Formulas and Worked Example

  • New pitch class = (original index + semitones) mod 12
  • Capo fret = semitones mod 12 (for upward transposition)
  • Written pitch (transposing instrument) = concert pitch + instrument offset

Worked example: transpose G major up 5 semitones:

  1. Find G on the chromatic circle: G = index 7 (C=0)
  2. Add 5: 7 + 5 = 12 → 12 mod 12 = 0 = C
  3. Interval name for +5: perfect fourth
  4. Chords move identically: G → C, C → F, D → G, Em → Am
  5. Capo alternative: fret the 5th fret and play G shapes to sound in C

Capo Math: The Two-Direction Trick

A capo raises pitch — fret 3 makes everything 3 semitones higher. That gives you two opposite superpowers. To play higher: keep your familiar chord shapes and clamp the capo at the transposition fret (G shapes + capo 2 = sounding in A). To stay in the same key with easier shapes: go the other way — want to sound in G but love C shapes? Capo 7 (G is 7 semitones above C) and play C shapes.

The mental shortcut: capo fret + shape key = sounding key (in semitones). Capo 5 + G shapes = C sounding. Rearranged: shape key = sounding key − capo fret. This is how session guitarists survive last-minute key changes — the singer wants it down a whole step, you slide the capo down two frets and change nothing else.

Transposing Instruments: The Conversion Chart

Some instruments are built in other keys: when a B♭ trumpet "plays C," concert pitch hears B♭. To write for them, transpose from concert pitch to their written pitch:

InstrumentTranspositionConcert C becomes (written)
Trumpet, clarinet, tenor saxB♭ (−2 semitones)D
Alto sax, baritone saxE♭ (−9 semitones)A
French hornF (−7 semitones)G
PiccoloOctave up (+12)C (8va)

Worked example: the band plays in concert B♭ major and you need a trumpet part. B♭ is index 10; add 2 → 12 mod 12 = 0 = C major written. The trumpeter reads C major and the audience hears B♭ — everyone wins. Reverse it to find concert pitch from a written part: subtract instead.

Enharmonic Spelling: When C♯ Becomes D♭

C♯ and D♭ are the same piano key but different spellings — and correct spelling matters for reading musicians. The rule of thumb: use sharps when transposing up, flats when transposing down, and keep the key signature sane (prefer 3 flats over 9 sharps). Transposing C up 1 semitone gives D♭ in flat-friendly contexts (jazz, brass) but C♯ in sharp-friendly ones (guitar, strings).

The calculator follows this convention automatically, and avoids theoretical keys (no G♯ major with its F𝄪) by respelling to the practical equivalent. If you're writing for specific players, match their instrument's tradition: brass reads flats, strings read sharps.

Transposition vs Modulation

Transposition moves an entire piece to a new key — everything shifts together. Modulation changes key within a piece, like the classic final-chorus lift up a whole step. DJs do live modulation with pitch faders; producers automate it. The math is identical (semitone shifts); the difference is scope — one moves the song, the other moves a section. The Nashville Number Shortcut

Session players often skip note names entirely with the Nashville Number System: chords become scale degrees (1, 4, 5, 6m). A I–V–vi–IV progression in G is G–D–Em–C; in C it's C–G–Am–F — same numbers, instant transposition. Learn progressions as numbers once, and you can play them in any key the calculator gives you without rewriting charts. It's transposition as a habit rather than a calculation.

Limitations

  • 12-tone equal temperament only. Microtonal music, just intonation, and historical temperaments need specialized tools.
  • Spelling is conventional, not universal. The sharp-up/flat-down rule covers 95% of cases; odd contexts may want the enharmonic alternative.
  • Range limits are yours to check. Transposing a trumpet part up a major third might exceed the instrument — the math doesn't know the player's range.
  • Capo past fret 7 shortens scale length enough to affect intonation on many guitars; consider retuning or a different voicing instead.
