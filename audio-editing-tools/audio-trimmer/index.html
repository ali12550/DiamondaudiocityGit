1. The Mathematics of Digital Audio Slicing

In digital signal processing, an audio signal is stored as a sequence of discrete numerical amplitude values (samples) captured at a uniform sampling frequency $f_s$ (commonly $44,100\text{ Hz}$ or $48,000\text{ Hz}$).

When you define a start time $t_{\text{start}}$ and end time $t_{\text{end}}$ in seconds, the audio engine translates these continuous time values into discrete sample indices:

Sample Index Conversion

n_{\text{start}} = \lfloor t_{\text{start}} \cdot f_s \rfloor
n_{\text{end}} = \lfloor t_{\text{end}} \cdot f_s \rfloor

Guarantees boundary precision down to approximately $22.7\ \mu\text{s}$ at $44.1\text{ kHz}$.

Buffer Extraction

L = n_{\text{end}} - n_{\text{start}}
y[m] = x[m + n_{\text{start}}] \quad (0 \le m < L)

Transfers channel data into an allocated sub-buffer without altering source audio.

2. Zero-Crossing Alignment & Preventing Boundary Pops

A universal challenge in digital audio editing is preventing loud "pops" or "clicks" at the boundary edges of trimmed clips.

Sound is created by oscillating air pressure translated into speaker cone displacement. If an audio file is severed while the waveform amplitude is non-zero (for example, at $+0.5\text{ V}$), the playback engine abruptly transitions to silence ($0\text{ V}$) in a single sample step.

According to Fourier analysis, an instantaneous step discontinuity represents a Heaviside step function whose derivative is a Dirac delta impulse $\delta(t)$—generating energy across all audible frequencies simultaneously, perceived as a sharp click:

Methods to Ensure Click-Free Edits:

  • Zero-Crossing Slicing: Always position cut points at the precise sample where the waveform crosses the zero-amplitude baseline ($x[n] = 0.0$).
  • Cosine Micro-Fades: Applying a microscopic 2–5 ms cosine or logarithmic fade-in at the beginning and fade-out at the end gently ramps voltage to zero, eliminating transient impulses completely.

3. Lossless PCM Extraction vs Lossy Re-Compression
