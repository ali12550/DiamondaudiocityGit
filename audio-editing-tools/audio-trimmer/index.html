What Is an Audio Trimmer?

An audio trimmer is a tool that removes the audio before a chosen start time and after a chosen end time, keeping only the selected segment as a new file. It works on decoded sample data, so trimming is a mathematical copy of a sample range — the original file is never re-recorded.

Trimming is the simplest edit in audio production and one of the most common. Podcasters cut pre-roll silence and false starts, musicians isolate drum breaks for samplers, and everyone has made a ringtone. Unlike fading (which changes volume) or normalizing (which changes level), trimming only changes duration: the kept samples are copied bit-for-bit from the decoded original.

How This Audio Trimmer Works

This tool trims audio entirely in your browser using the Web Audio API — decoding, cutting, and WAV encoding all happen on your device, with no server involved.

Methodology, per the tool's processing code: the uploaded file is decoded into 32-bit floating-point PCM at its native sample rate. Your start and end times are converted to sample indices with n = ⌊t × fs⌋. A new buffer is created and the samples in the range are copied channel by channel, untouched. A preview is played directly from the selection, and the download is encoded as a standard 44-byte-header, 16-bit PCM RIFF WAV. We checked the WAV writer's header fields against the RIFF specification and verified the sample-index math against hand calculation for five start/end pairs.

Trimming Formula and Worked Example

Every trim is two index calculations and one copy:

nstart = ⌊tstart × fs⌋, nend = ⌊tend × fs⌋, y[m] = x[m + nstart]

Worked example: trim a podcast from 1:15.50 to 2:00.00 at 44.1 kHz:

  1. Start in seconds: 1 × 60 + 15.50 = 75.50 s → nstart = ⌊75.50 × 44100⌋ = 3,329,550
  2. End in seconds: 120.00 s → nend = ⌊120 × 44100⌋ = 5,292,000
  3. Clip length: 5,292,000 − 3,329,550 = 1,962,450 samples (44.50 s)
  4. Export size (stereo 16-bit WAV): 1,962,450 × 2 channels × 2 bytes ≈ 7.8 MB

Sample precision is why the seconds inputs accept one decimal place: at 44.1 kHz, 0.1 s is 4,410 samples — plenty fine for dialogue, though frame-level beats for music should use the waveform view to land on beat transients.

Why Trimmed Clips Click — and How to Fix It

Clicks at a cut point have a physical cause: if the waveform is cut while it is far from zero amplitude, playback jumps from that value to silence (or to the next clip's value) in a single sample step. A sudden vertical jump contains energy at every frequency at once, which your ear hears as a sharp click or pop.

Two standard fixes exist. Zero-crossing cuts place the boundary exactly where the waveform crosses the zero line, so the jump is tiny. Micro-fades (2–5 ms fades at the very start and end) ramp the edge to zero so smoothly that no click can form. Be honest about this tool's behavior: it performs raw sample-boundary cuts with no automatic fade, so for the cleanest result, zoom into the waveform and place your start and end points where the wave sits near the center line. If a clip still clicks, re-export with the cut nudged a few milliseconds toward a quiet moment.

Formats, Quality, and the No-Recompression Rule

Most free online cutters decode your MP3, cut it, and re-encode it as MP3 — running the psychoacoustic compressor a second time and smearing transients. This tool skips that pass entirely: decode once to full-resolution PCM, cut, and write 16-bit WAV. The result cannot be better than the source, but it is exactly as good as the source.

Making a Ringtone From a Song

Ringtones are the most-searched reason people trim audio, and the iPhone rules are strict: a custom iPhone ringtone must be 30 seconds or shorter, in AAC format, with a .m4r extension (per Macworld's ringtone guide). Longer clips are rejected when you sync.

Workflow: trim the catchiest 25–30 seconds of the song here, download the WAV, convert it to AAC/M4A in any converter or DAW, and rename the extension from .m4a to .m4r. Then drag it onto your iPhone in Finder (macOS) or iTunes (Windows) — it appears under Settings → Sounds & Haptics → Ringtone. Android phones generally accept MP3 or OGG ringtones of any reasonable length, so the trimmed WAV converted to MP3 works directly.

Limitations

  • No automatic zero-crossing snap or fades. Cuts are raw sample boundaries — place cut points near the waveform's center line for click-free results.
  • WAV export only. The download is always 16-bit PCM WAV; for MP3 or AAC, convert afterward in another app.
  • Seconds inputs cap at 59 per the widget design; use the minutes field for selections longer than a minute.
  • Everything lives in RAM. The full decoded file sits in memory, so multi-hour recordings are best trimmed on a desktop browser.
  • Preview plays the selection only. What you hear in Preview is the exact range that will be exported — no more, no less.
