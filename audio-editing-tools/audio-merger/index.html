Seamlessly combine multiple audio tracks, voiceover recordings, podcasts, and musical movements into a single high-fidelity audio stream. Explore the signal processing mechanics of PCM concatenation, buffer alignment, sample-rate interpolation, and click-free boundary transitions.

1. How Digital Audio Concatenation Works

In digital signal processing (DSP), audio concatenation (often called track joining or splicing) is the sequential appending of time-domain audio buffers along a single continuous timeline. This differs fundamentally from audio summing or mixing:

Audio Summing (Mixing)

Simultaneous signals played together at identical sample timestamps:

y[n] = x_1[n] + x_2[n] + ... + x_k[n]

Requires amplitude attenuation to avoid clipping when combined voltages exceed 0 dBFS.

Audio Concatenation (Splicing)

Sequential signals aligned end-to-end along the time axis:

y[n] = x_1[n] \quad (0 \le n < N_1), \quad x_2[n - N_1] \quad (N_1 \le n < N_1 + N_2)

Preserves independent dynamic ranges without causing intermodulation or summing saturation.

When you combine audio files using Pitch Detectors Audio Merger, the entire decoding and concatenation pipeline occurs locally inside your web browser via the standardized Web Audio API. No raw media is transmitted over the internet, ensuring maximum processing speed, zero bandwidth overhead, and complete privacy for sensitive studio material and voice recordings.

3. Eliminating Boundary Pops & Zero-Crossing Dynamics

A common artifact in amateur audio editing is an audible click or snap at the exact seam where two audio files meet. This artifact stems from two physical phenomena:

  • Discontinuous Waveform Jumps: If Track 1 terminates while the waveform is at $+0.5\text{ V}$ and Track 2 commences at $-0.4\text{ V}$, the speaker diaphragm is commanded to travel across a step voltage of $0.9\text{ V}$ in an infinitesimally small time step ($Delta t = 1/f_s \approx 22.7\ \mu\text{s}$). This step discontinuity generates high-frequency harmonic energy spanning the entire audio spectrum.
  • DC Offset: An asymmetrical bias in recording equipment can shift the resting equilibrium away from $0\text{ V}$. Splicing two files with differing DC offsets introduces an instantaneous baseline jump.

Best Practices for Seamless Audio Splicing:

  1. Zero-Crossing Alignment: Trim audio clips at the exact sample index where amplitude crosses $y[n] = 0.0$.
  2. Micro-Fades (2–10 ms): Applying a cosine-shaped fade-out to the tail of Clip 1 and a fade-in to the head of Clip 2 guarantees smooth voltage continuity down to zero.
  3. Equal-Power Crossfading: When overlapping ambient audio beds, apply an equal-power curve ($L(t) = \cos(\theta), R(t) = \sin(\theta)$) to maintain constant acoustic energy without a 3 dB dip in perceived loudness.

The tool arranges these sample arrays sequentially in a new AudioBuffer and encodes the combined samples into a standard 16-bit RIFF WAV file locally on your CPU.

Because the output is uncompressed, no second lossy perceptual encoding pass occurs, avoiding the artifact accumulation common in traditional re-encoding pipelines.

If you combine mono (1 channel) and stereo (2 channels) tracks, the mono channels are duplicated across left and right channels to maintain consistent stereo panning throughout the merged timeline.
