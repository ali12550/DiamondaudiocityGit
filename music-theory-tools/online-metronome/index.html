What Is an Online Metronome?

An online metronome is a free tempo-keeping tool that plays precise clicks at a set beats-per-minute rate, right in your browser. Choose a BPM and time signature, press play, and it marks every beat — with a distinct accent on beat 1 — while flashing visual beat indicators in sync.

Musicians use it to build rock-solid timing, learn difficult passages at reduced tempos, and keep ensembles together. Unlike a phone app, it needs no install and works on any device with a browser and speakers.

How This Metronome Works

This metronome generates clicks with a timer-driven scheduler in your browser. The exact mechanics:

  1. Set the tempo: the slider spans 20–300 BPM; the ±1/±10 buttons fine-tune it.
  2. Pick a meter: time-signature buttons (2/4 through 7/8) set how many clicks form a bar.
  3. Press play: a repeating timer fires each beat — a 1,000 Hz tone on beat 1, an 800 Hz click on the rest — while the beat dots flash in sync.
  4. Read the telemetry: milliseconds-per-beat and seconds-per-bar are computed live for DAW sync work.

Beat interval follows ms per beat = 60,000 ÷ BPM — at 120 BPM each click lands exactly 500 ms apart.

Tempo Math and Worked Example

Two formulas govern every metronome:

beat interval (ms) = 60,000 ÷ BPM · bar duration (s) = beats per bar × 60 ÷ BPM

Worked example — 96 BPM in 4/4:

  1. Beat interval: 60,000 ÷ 96 = 625 ms between clicks.
  2. Bar duration: 4 × 60 ÷ 96 = 2.5 seconds per bar.
  3. A 32-bar practice loop: 32 × 2.5 = 80 seconds per repetition.

How to Use the Online Metronome

  1. Dial your BPM: drag the slider or tap ±1/±10 for precision.
  2. Choose the meter: 4/4 for most music, 3/4 for waltzes, 6/8 for compound feels, 5/4 or 7/8 for odd meters.
  3. Press the center play button: the downbeat accent (higher pitch) marks beat 1 of each bar.
  4. Play along: start with simple material at a comfortable tempo, then raise the BPM in small steps.

Why 20–300 BPM? The Maelzel Scale Story

The metronome's range comes from Johann Maelzel's 1815 mechanical metronome, whose pendulum scale ran 40–208 BPM. Digital metronomes kept the familiar span and extended it: 20 BPM for extreme slow practice, 300 for blast beats and speed drills.

Below ~40 BPM, most musicians stop feeling the pulse and start counting subdivisions instead — which is exactly what the subdivision practice below is for. Above ~208, individual clicks blur together, and players feel the half-time pulse instead.

Subdivisions: The Secret to Actually Improving

The fastest way to fix shaky timing is to subdivide: hear 8th or 16th notes instead of quarters. Quarter-note clicks let you rush the gaps unnoticed; subdivisions put a click on every slice of time, exposing exactly where you drag or hurry.

Practice method: set the metronome to half tempo with double subdivisions — e.g. 60 BPM clicking 8ths instead of 120 BPM clicking quarters. Same absolute time, twice the information. When 16ths feel easy, return to quarters: the steadiness transfers. Our rhythm pattern maker builds subdivision exercises on the same principle.

The Timing Accuracy Question, Answered Honestly

Browser metronomes using JavaScript timers are accurate enough for practice, but not sample-perfect. This tool schedules clicks with a repeating timer, which can jitter by a few milliseconds when the browser is busy — inaudible while practicing, but not suitable for scientific measurement.

True sample-accurate timing uses the Web Audio clock (AudioContext.currentTime), which runs on high-priority audio threads. For practice, recording, and rehearsal, the difference is academic: your own timing variance dwarfs any timer jitter. For DAW-grade click tracks, render the click in your DAW instead.

Odd Meters: Counting 5/4 and 7/8 Without Fear

Odd meters are just familiar chunks glued together. Count 5/4 as 3+2 ("ONE-two-three-FOUR-five") and 7/8 as 2+2+3 or 3+2+2 — the metronome's accent always marks beat 1, so your job is grouping the rest.

Start at half tempo with the accent loud in your mind, clap the grouping before touching your instrument, and only then play. Most musicians learn 7/8 in a single focused session this way; the fear is bigger than the difficulty.

Limitations

  • Timer-based, not sample-accurate: a few milliseconds of jitter is possible under heavy browser load — fine for practice, not for measurement.
  • No subdivision clicks: the tool clicks beats only; subdivide mentally or use the rhythm pattern maker.
  • Fixed accent pattern: beat 1 is always accented — music with displaced accents needs a programmable click.
  • Sound depends on your device: laptop speakers smear transients; use headphones or monitors for the tightest feel.
