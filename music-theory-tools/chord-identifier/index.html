Tertian Harmony: How Chords Are Built

Western harmonic theory is fundamentally tertian, meaning chords are constructed by stacking thirds on top of a foundational root note. A third is a musical interval spanning either three semitones (minor third, $m3$) or four semitones (major third, $M3$). The precise combination of stacked thirds defines chord quality:

Major Triad

M3 + m3 (4 + 3 st)

Formula: $1 - 3 - 5$
Acoustic Quality: Bright, consonant, stable.

Minor Triad

m3 + M3 (3 + 4 st)

Formula: $1 - lat 3 - 5$
Acoustic Quality: Somber, contemplative, melancholic.

Diminished Triad

m3 + m3 (3 + 3 st)

Formula: $1 - lat 3 - lat 5$
Acoustic Quality: Tense, restless, unstable tritone.

Augmented Triad

M3 + M3 (4 + 4 st)

Formula: $1 - 3 - sharp 5$
Acoustic Quality: Dreamy, floating, symmetrical.

Chord Inversions & Slash Notation

A chord's identity is defined by its constituent pitch classes, but its emotional weight and harmonic drive are dictated by which note is voiced in the bass (the lowest sounding pitch). Rearranging the vertical order of notes produces inversions:

First Inversion Third ($3$) $E - G - C$ C/E $6/3$ Smooth scalar stepwise bass movement
Second Inversion Fifth ($5$) $G - C - E$ C/G $6/4$ Cadential anticipation ($I^6_4 \to V \to I$) or pedal point

Why Symmetrical Chords Have Multiple Names

One of the most fascinating aspects of music theory is enharmonic equivalence. When chord intervals divide the 12-semitone octave equally, the chord becomes symmetrical and can be theoretically named from any of its notes:

Fully Diminished 7th Chords

Divides the 12-semitone octave into four equal minor thirds ($3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12\text{ st}$).

The pitch set C - Eb - F# - A represents:
• $C^{\circ 7}$ (resolves to $D\flat$)
• $E\flat^{\circ 7}$ (resolves to $E$)
• $F\sharp^{\circ 7}$ (resolves to $G$)
• $A^{\circ 7}$ (resolves to $B\flat$)

Augmented Triads

Divides the octave into three equal major thirds ($4 + 4 + 4 = 12\text{ st}$).

The pitch set C - E - G# is simultaneously:
• $C\text{aug}$ (resolves to $F$)
• $E\text{aug}$ (resolves to $A$)
• $A\flat\text{aug}$ ($G\sharp\text{aug}$, resolves to $D\flat$)

Step-by-Step Worked Chord Identification Examples

Example 1: Identifying a Mysterious Guitar Voicing

Notes Played: A guitarist plays an acoustic voicing containing notes: D, F#, A, C, E. What chord is this?

  1. Identify root candidates: Root $= D$.
  2. Compute intervals from $D$:
    • $D = 0$ (Root $1$)
    • $F\sharp = 4$ (Major 3rd)
    • $A = 7$ (Perfect 5th)
    • $C = 10$ (Minor 7th)
    • $E = 14$ or $2$ (Major 9th)
  3. Evaluate structure: $1 - 3 - 5 - \flat 7 - 9$.
Identified Chord: D9 (Dominant 9th). An indispensable staple of blues, jazz, and classic funk grooves.
Example 2: Resolving Am7 vs C6 Ambiguity

Notes Played: A keyboardist holds C, E, G, A with $C$ in the left-hand bass.

  1. Perspective A (Root $= C$): $C(1), E(3), G(5), A(6) \implies$ C6 (Major 6th).
  2. Perspective B (Root $= A$): $A(1), C(\flat 3), E(5), G(\flat 7) \implies$ Am7 in First Inversion (Am7/C).
Theoretical Resolution: If the song resolves cleanly to $C$ major, it functions as C6. If moving toward $D\text{m7} \to G7$, it functions as Am7/C (the vi chord leading to ii-V).
