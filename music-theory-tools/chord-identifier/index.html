A chord identifier is a reverse chord finder: enter the notes you are playing and it names the chord — its root, quality, interval formula, and inversion. Select any combination of pitches above and get an instant, theory-grade identification.

Key Takeaways

  • A chord identifier names any chord from its notes — select 3+ pitches and get the chord name, quality, interval formula, and inversion.
  • Chords are identified by interval patterns: C–E–G is always a major triad (4 + 3 semitones), whatever the octave or order.

What Is a Chord Identifier?

A chord identifier is a reverse chord finder: you enter the notes you are playing, and it tells you the chord's name. Instead of looking up "how do I play Cmaj9," you work backwards from a voicing you discovered — on piano, guitar, or in your DAW — to its formal name, quality, and interval formula.

This "reverse" direction is what makes it a songwriter's tool. Composers constantly stumble onto beautiful voicings by ear without knowing their names; the identifier bridges that gap, giving you the theory label you need to communicate the chord, transpose it with our key transposition tool, or reuse it in a progression.

How This Calculator Works

The widget above identifies chords by exhaustive root analysis — the same method a theorist uses, automated. Here is the exact procedure:

  1. Try every note as root: each selected pitch is tested as the potential root in turn.
  2. Measure intervals: the remaining notes are converted to semitone distances above that candidate root (modulo 12, so octaves collapse).
  3. Match templates: each interval pattern is compared against a library of chord formulas — triads, sevenths, ninths, suspensions, and more.
  4. Report all matches: every root/template combination that fits is listed, with the interval formula (e.g. 0 – 4 – 7 – 11) and the inversion implied by your lowest note.

Because every candidate root is tested, the tool never forces a single answer on ambiguous voicings — it shows you the alternatives and lets your ear decide.

Chord Formula and Worked Example

Every chord is an interval recipe measured in semitones from the root:

major triad = 0 – 4 – 7 (root, major third, perfect fifth)

Worked example: you select C, E, G, B on the note picker.

  1. Testing C as root: intervals are 0 (C), 4 (E), 7 (G), 11 (B) → matches the major-seventh template → Cmaj7.
  2. Testing E as root: intervals are 0 (E), 3 (G), 8 (B), 9 (C) → no standard template matches cleanly.
  3. Other roots similarly fail to match, so the tool reports Cmaj7 as the primary reading, formula 0 – 4 – 7 – 11.
  4. If your lowest selected note were E, the same pitch set would be reported as Cmaj7/E — first inversion.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Select notes: click pitches on the interactive note selector (choose at least 3; order doesn't matter).
  2. Read the chord name: the primary identification appears instantly, with the chord quality (major, minor, diminished…).
  3. Check the formula: the interval pattern (e.g. 0 – 3 – 7) shows you the chord's anatomy in semitones.
  4. Review alternatives: open the alternative readings — different roots can explain the same notes.
  5. Hear it: press play to audition the voicing and confirm it matches what you imagined.

Why One Voicing Has Three Names

The most confusing — and most useful — thing a chord identifier teaches is that chord names are interpretations, not facts. C–E–G–B is Cmaj7 with C in the bass, but the pitch set C–E–G–A is both Am7 (A–C–E–G) and C6 (C–E–G–A). Same four notes, two legitimate names, different harmonic functions.

Rule of thumb: the name follows the bass note and context. If the bassist plays A, call it Am7; if the progression resolves to F, C6 probably describes its job better. The tool's alternative readings exist precisely so you can pick the name that matches your music — then use the interval calculator to double-check any single interval you're unsure about.

Inversions: The Bass Note Changes Everything

An inversion is the same chord with a different note in the bass. C–E–G with C lowest is root position; with E lowest it is first inversion (written C/E); with G lowest, second inversion (C/G). The chord's identity doesn't change, but its stability and voice-leading do — second-inversion triads sound restless and "need" to resolve, which is why composers use them at cadences.

Because the widget reads your lowest selected note as the bass, selection order matters for the inversion label (though not for the chord name). Building the voicing low-to-high exactly as you play it gives the most useful reading.

From a Chord to a Full Progression

A named chord is a starting point — progressions are the destination. Once the identifier names your voicing, take its root and quality into the chord progression generator: a Cmaj7 discovery becomes the I chord of a I–vi–ii–V in C, and the generator shows you the diatonic companions that will make it sound intentional rather than accidental.

Producers use this loop constantly: hum a voicing into a piano roll, identify it, generate three progressions around it, and pick the one whose emotional arc fits the track. Theory serves the ear — never the reverse.

Limitations

  • Needs at least 3 notes. Two notes make an interval, not a chord — use the interval calculator for dyads.
  • 12-tone equal temperament assumed. Microtonal or just-intonation voicings will be rounded to the nearest semitone template.
  • Context-free naming. The tool names the pitch set, not its function in your song — Am7 vs C6 is ultimately your call.
  • Cluster voicings may match nothing. Dense chromatic clusters (e.g. C–C♯–D) have no standard chord name, and the tool will honestly say so.
