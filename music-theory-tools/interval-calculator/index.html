Musical Interval Calculator: Pitch Distance & Harmonic Ratio Analysis

Calculate the precise musical interval between any two pitches. Explore semitone counts, diatonic and chromatic classifications, the Rule of 9 interval inversion, and acoustic frequency ratios.

In music theory, an interval is the fundamental building block of all melody and harmony. When two notes sound successively, they create a melodic interval that guides linear phrasing. When two notes sound simultaneously, they produce a harmonic interval that dictates acoustic consonance or dissonance. From the physical physics of vibrating strings to the emotional color of cinematic film scores, understanding intervals allows musicians to construct chords, transpose melodies, and develop keen ear training skills.

The Anatomy of a Musical Interval

Every musical interval possesses two distinct descriptors: Numerical Size (generic distance) and Quality (tonal character).

1. Numerical Size (Scale Distance)

Calculated by counting the total number of letter names from the first note to the second note, inclusive of both. For example, the distance from $C$ to $E$ is a third ($C-1, D-2, E-3$), regardless of whether accidentals (sharps or flats) are present.

2. Interval Quality (Semitone Count)

Determined by the exact number of chromatic semitones (half-steps) separating the two pitches. Unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves are categorized as Perfect, while 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths are categorized as Major or Minor.

The Rule of 9: Inversion Relationships

Inverting an interval means shifting the lower note up an octave so it becomes the higher note. Interval inversions follow mathematical laws:

Numerical Rule: Sum = 9
  • Unison ($1$) inverts to an Octave ($8$): $1 + 8 = 9$
  • Second ($2$) inverts to a Seventh ($7$): $2 + 7 = 9$
  • Third ($3$) inverts to a Sixth ($6$): $3 + 6 = 9$
  • Fourth ($4$) inverts to a Fifth ($5$): $4 + 5 = 9$
Quality Inversion Rule
  • Major inverts to Minor (and vice versa)
  • Augmented inverts to Diminished (and vice versa)
  • Perfect remains Perfect

Acoustic Physics: Why Do Certain Intervals Sound Consonant?

The human sensation of musical consonance vs. dissonance is physically rooted in the overlap of harmonic overtones. When two pitches share simple small-integer frequency ratios, their respective harmonic series align without physical interference:

The Perfect 5th ($3:2$ Ratio): Every second harmonic of the upper note aligns precisely with every third harmonic of the lower note. The waveforms reinforce each other smoothly, producing zero acoustic beating.

The Minor 2nd ($16:15$ Ratio): The fundamental frequencies are so close ($f_2 - f_1 \approx 10\text{--}30\text{ Hz}$) that they fall within the same critical band of the human cochlea. This generates rapid amplitude modulation (acoustic beating) perceived as harsh friction and dissonance.

Frequently Asked Questions

A musical interval is the distance in pitch between two notes. If the notes sound simultaneously, it is a harmonic interval; if they sound sequentially in time, it is a melodic interval. Every interval is defined by its numerical generic size (second, third, fourth) and its quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented, or diminished).

Unisons, octaves, fourths, and fifths are called 'Perfect' because of their high acoustic consonance and simple harmonic ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3). When inverted, a perfect interval remains perfect (a perfect 4th inverts to a perfect 5th). In contrast, thirds and sixths exist naturally in two distinct forms (major and minor) and invert between each other.

The Rule of 9 states that when an interval is inverted (moving the lower note up an octave), the original interval size and its inversion always add up to 9: 2nds become 7ths, 3rds become 6ths, 4ths become 5ths, and unisons become octaves. Qualities also invert: Major becomes Minor, Minor becomes Major, Augmented becomes Diminished, and Perfect remains Perfect.

The tritone is an interval spanning exactly 3 whole tones or 6 semitones (an augmented fourth or diminished fifth). It splits the 12-semitone octave exactly in half. In medieval music theory, it was termed 'the devil in music' due to its intense acoustic dissonance, instability, and strict prohibition in liturgical counterpoint.

A compound interval is any interval larger than one octave (greater than 12 semitones). It is calculated by adding 7 to the simple interval: a 9th is a compound 2nd (8 + 2 semitones = 14 st), an 11th is a compound 4th (17 st), and a 13th is a compound 6th (21 st). Compound intervals form the basis of extended jazz chords.

Musicians associate intervals with the opening notes of famous songs to recognize pitch distances instantly. For example, a minor 2nd is the 'Jaws' theme, a perfect 4th is 'Here Comes the Bride', a perfect 5th is the 'Star Wars' theme, and an octave is 'Somewhere Over the Rainbow'.
