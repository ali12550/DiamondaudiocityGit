What Is a Melody Generator?

A melody generator is an interactive step sequencer that lets you compose melodies by clicking notes onto a grid. Each column is a time step, each row is a pitch from your chosen scale — click cells to place notes, press play, and hear your melody looped at your chosen tempo with a synthesized instrument.

Unlike random melody tools, this one is compositional, not generative: every note is your choice, but the grid only offers in-scale pitches, so nothing you click can clash with your key. It is a melody sketchpad that removes the fear of wrong notes.

How This Calculator Works

The widget above is a browser-based step sequencer running on the Web Audio API. The exact mechanics:

  1. Choose a scale and root: the grid's rows remap to the selected scale's pitches (e.g. C major: C–D–E–F–G–A–B across two octaves).
  2. Click cells: each filled cell schedules one note at that step; empty cells are rests. Multiple filled cells in one column play as a chord.
  3. Set tempo and instrument: the tempo slider (60–200 BPM) sets step duration; the instrument selector changes the oscillator timbre.
  4. Press play: a scheduler walks the columns left to right, triggering Web Audio oscillators at each step's frequency — looping until you stop it.

Because the grid only contains scale tones, every possible click pattern is diatonic — the tool constrains pitch so you can focus entirely on rhythm and contour.

Melody Math and Worked Example

The timing math behind the grid is simple division:

step duration (seconds) = 60 ÷ BPM ÷ steps per beat

Worked example: 16-step grid at 120 BPM with 4 steps per beat (16th notes).

  1. One beat: 60 ÷ 120 = 0.5 seconds.
  2. One step: 0.5 ÷ 4 = 0.125 seconds (125 ms).
  3. One full 16-step loop: 16 × 0.125 = 2.0 seconds = one 4/4 bar.
  4. Frequencies come from equal temperament: A4 = 440 Hz, each semitone multiplies by 21/12.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Pick a scale and root: start with major for bright hooks or natural minor for darker ones.
  2. Set the tempo: match your project's BPM (find it with BPM tap tempo).
  3. Click a rhythm: fill 4–8 cells in one row first — rhythm before pitch.
  4. Assign pitches: move some notes to different rows, keeping the rhythm. Small moves (neighboring scale degrees) sound most natural.
  5. Loop and refine: press play, listen on repeat, and adjust one note at a time until it sticks in your head.

Why Constraint Creates Better Melodies

Beginners write better melodies with 7 allowed notes than with 88. This is the paradox the grid exploits: unlimited choice produces random wandering, while a scale constraint forces you to make music from rhythm, repetition, and contour — the actual ingredients of memorable melody. Jazz improvisers practice the same way, restricting themselves to one scale until it sings.

Try the experiment: compose one 16-step pattern using all rows, then one using only 3 rows. The 3-row version will almost always sound more like a "real" hook. Limitation is a compositional technique, not a training wheel.

The 3-Note Hook Rule

The most memorable melodies in pop history use shockingly few distinct pitches. Think of hooks you can whistle: most live on 3–4 scale degrees, repeated with rhythmic variation. The brain latches onto small pitch sets because it can predict them — and prediction fulfilled feels like catchiness.

Practical method: fill your grid using only the root, third, and fifth of your scale (the 1–3–5 of the tonic triad). Write 8 steps. Then allow yourself one outside pitch as an accent. That single surprise note against a stable 3-note core is the entire craft of hook writing in miniature.

Rhythm First, Pitches Second

Professional songwriters compose rhythm before pitch. The groove of a melody — where notes fall against the beat — determines its feel far more than which notes they are. A great rhythm on one repeated pitch already sounds like music; great pitches with dead rhythm sound like an exercise.

Grid workflow: click 6–8 cells all in the same row until the rhythm grooves on loop. Only then start moving individual notes to new rows. You will find the melody almost writes itself — because the hard part (rhythm) is already solved.

From Grid to DAW: Finishing Your Melody

The grid is a sketchpad; your DAW is the canvas. Once a pattern loops well, recreate it in your DAW's piano roll by ear (16 steps at 120 BPM = one bar — count it in), then develop it: add a second phrase that answers the first, vary the ending, and harmonize it with chords from the chord progression generator.

Check your melody's note set against the scale finder if you plan key changes, and verify any sung version sits in a comfortable range with the vocal range finder.

Limitations

  • Diatonic only: chromatic passing tones and blue notes aren't on the grid — add them in your DAW.
  • Fixed grid resolution: triplets and swung rhythms can't be represented on an even step grid.
  • Synth preview, not production audio: the Web Audio timbres sketch the idea; final sound design happens in your DAW.
  • No dynamics: every step plays at equal velocity — accents and phrasing are up to you.
