What Is a Rhythm Pattern Maker?

A rhythm pattern maker is an online step sequencer that lets you program drum grooves by toggling steps on a grid. Each column is a slice of musical time and each row is a drum sound; the pattern loops continuously. This free tool gives you a 16-step grid (expandable to 32 or 64), tempo and swing controls, classic drum kits, and one-click export.

The step sequencer is one of the oldest ideas in electronic music: divide a bar into equal steps, decide which steps trigger a sound, and loop it. This tool brings that workflow to your browser with sample-accurate timing.

Key Takeaways

  • A 16-step grid = one bar of 16th notes in 4/4 time; steps 1, 5, 9, and 13 are the quarter-note downbeats.
  • Each step's length in milliseconds is 15,000 ÷ BPM — at 120 BPM every step lasts exactly 125 ms.
  • Swing delays the off-beat steps; 54–62% gives the classic MPC hip-hop groove, 66.7% equals a triplet shuffle.
  • Start from genre blueprints (four-on-the-floor, boom-bap, tresillo) instead of an empty grid.
  • Export your pattern as JSON and rebuild it in any DAW in under a minute.

How This Rhythm Pattern Maker Works

Step Timing Math and Worked Example

Every step sequencer runs on one formula. In 4/4 time, one bar holds 4 quarter-note beats, and each beat divides into 4 sixteenth notes — 16 steps per bar. The time each step occupies is:

Step duration (ms) = 15,000 ÷ BPM

(Because one quarter note lasts 60,000 ÷ BPM milliseconds, and a sixteenth note is one quarter of that.) Here is what that means at common tempos:

TempoTypical genreOne step lastsOne bar lasts
92 BPMBoom-bap hip-hop163.0 ms2.61 s
120 BPMPop / funk125.0 ms2.00 s
128 BPMHouse117.2 ms1.88 s
140 BPMTrap (half-time feel)107.1 ms1.71 s
174 BPMDrum and bass86.2 ms1.38 s

Worked example: at 128 BPM, one step = 15,000 ÷ 128 = 117.2 ms. A four-on-the-floor kick on steps 1, 5, 9, and 13 lands every 4 × 117.2 = 468.8 ms — exactly one quarter-note apart.

Reading Your Pattern: Where the Beats Fall

On a 16-step grid in 4/4, not all steps are equal. Steps 1, 5, 9, and 13 are the quarter-note downbeats — the pulse you nod your head to. Steps 3, 7, 11, and 15 are the "ands" between beats, where off-beat hi-hats live. Everything else is sixteenth-note detail.

1, 5, 9, 13Quarter-note downbeatsKick drum foundation
5, 13Beats 2 and 4Snare / clap backbeat
3, 7, 11, 15Off-beats ("ands")Open hats, syncopation
2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16Sixteenth subdivisionsHi-hat drive, ghost notes, rolls

Genre Groove Blueprints: Copy These Step Patterns

Start from a genre's rhythmic DNA, then change 2–3 steps to make it yours:

  • House / Techno (124–128 BPM): kick on 1, 5, 9, 13 (four-on-the-floor); clap on 5, 13; open hats on 3, 7, 11, 15; closed hats on every even step.
  • Boom-bap hip-hop (90–94 BPM): kick on 1, 7, 10; snare on 5, 13; hats on every step with swing at 56–58%. The lazy kick placement behind the grid is the whole sound.
  • Trap (140 BPM, half-time feel): kick on 1, 8, 11; snare on 9 only (the "third-beat" snap); rapid hat rolls on 14–16 using the Triplet 16 division.
  • Dembow / Reggaeton (~100 BPM): kick on 1, 4, 7, 10, 13; snare on the tresillo accents — steps 4, 7, and 12. That 3+3+2 kick skeleton is non-negotiable.
  • Drum and bass (172–176 BPM): kick on 1, 11; snare on 5, 13; two-step hats shuffling on the off-beats. Speed does the work — keep the pattern sparse.

Swing Percentage Translated to Milliseconds

Swing delays every even-numbered step by a fraction of the step duration. The offset formula is:

Swing offset (ms) = (Swing% ÷ 100) × Step duration (ms)

Worked example: at 120 BPM with 58% swing, one step lasts 125 ms. Each off-beat step shifts later by 0.58 × 125 = 72.5 ms. That 72 ms delay is the difference between a robotic grid and a head-nodding groove — long enough to feel, short enough to stay locked.

Reference points: 0% is machine-straight; 54–62% is the classic MPC hip-hop pocket; 66.7% equals a true triplet shuffle (2:1 ratio).

From Browser to DAW: The Export Workflow

The Export JSON button downloads your entire session — grid triggers, BPM, swing, kit, and pattern length — as a small text file. The JSON maps each drum voice to an array of active step numbers, for example {"kick": [1, 5, 9, 13], "bpm": 128}. To continue in a DAW:

  1. Export the JSON and open it in any text editor to see your step numbers.
  2. In your DAW's drum sequencer (Ableton Drum Rack, FL Studio Channel Rack, or Logic's Step Sequencer), set the same BPM and step count.
  3. Enable the matching step numbers for each drum voice — the numbers transfer 1:1.
  4. Re-apply swing in the DAW (most offer a groove/swing knob) using the percentage you dialed in here.

Keep the JSON files as versioned sketches (chorus-v1.json, chorus-v2.json) — they weigh kilobytes, and Import JSON reloads any of them for quick auditioning.

Choosing a Pattern Length for Song Sections

Pattern length is an arrangement decision, not just a bigger grid. 16 steps (1 bar) is for sketching and for sections that must feel hypnotic — techno intros, trap verses. 32 steps (2 bars) lets you write call-and-response: keep bar one simple, add a fill or variation in bar two. 64 steps (4 bars) fits a full phrase with an intro, build, and turnaround — ideal for choruses and drops.

A practical workflow: sketch the verse at 16 steps, expand to 32 for the pre-chorus with one added fill, and open the full 64 for the chorus. For bar-to-time conversions at your tempo, use the BPM to milliseconds calculator.

Polyrhythm vs. Polymeter: Stop Mixing Them Up

A polyrhythm layers two subdivisions over the same timespan — 3 evenly spaced hits in the time of 2 beats (the 3:2 hemiola). A polymeter layers two different bar lengths sharing the same step duration — a 4-step loop against a 5-step loop, realigning every 20 steps (their lowest common multiple).

On this grid, accent steps 1, 6, and 11 against straight quarter-note kicks for a 3-against-4 polyrhythm. For a polymeter, loop a 12-step phrase inside the 16-step grid by muting one voice's last 4 steps and let the downbeat drift.

Limitations

  • Browser audio latency varies. The Transport clock keeps timing tight, but Bluetooth headphones and heavy system load can add 20–100 ms of output delay. Use wired headphones for critical groove work.
  • Export is JSON, not audio. This tool saves patterns, not rendered WAV files — you rebuild the sound in a DAW or re-record the browser output.
  • No per-step velocity (yet). Every hit plays at full volume, so ghost notes and accents must be programmed with density rather than dynamics.
  • Fixed 4/4 framework. Odd meters like 7/8 or 5/4 can't be represented on the 16-step grid; approximate them with muted steps or use a DAW.
  • Two kits only. The Classic and 505 kits cover a lot of ground, but they won't replace a full sample library for acoustic genres.
