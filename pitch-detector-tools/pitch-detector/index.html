What Is a Pitch Detector?

Definition: A pitch detector is a real-time audio analysis tool that measures a sound's fundamental frequency and converts it into musical information. It samples the microphone thousands of times per second, finds the waveform's repetition rate, and maps it to the nearest note of the chromatic scale plus the cent deviation from perfect tuning.

Singers check intonation during practice; instrumentalists use them as tuners; teachers use them for ear training, since students can see the note they sang and how far it drifted. Browser-based detectors need no app download — the Web Audio API gives any modern browser the raw microphone data the math needs.

How This Pitch Detector Works

This detector grabs short slices of your microphone audio, measures how fast the waveform repeats, and turns that rate into a note name, a frequency, and a cents reading.

Your browser requests the microphone with getUserMedia and routes the stream into the Web Audio API. An analyser node captures 2,048-sample slices — about 43 milliseconds at the common 48 kHz sample rate. A normalized autocorrelation algorithm compares each slice against time-shifted copies of itself; the shift with the strongest match is the wave's period (τ), and the fundamental frequency is f₀ = sample rate ÷ τ. This time-domain approach stays accurate on low notes, where FFT analysis suffers from spectral leakage.

The tool maps f₀ to the chromatic scale at A4 = 440 Hz with the MIDI formula n = 69 + 12 × log₂(f₀ ÷ 440). Rounding n gives the nearest note; the leftover fraction becomes the cent deviation: cents = 1200 × log₂(f₀ ÷ target). Two guardrails keep the display honest: sounds quieter than an RMS level of 0.01 are rejected (the display shows "--"), and only 50–2000 Hz is reported. The integer-multiple harmonics riding on your fundamental are ignored — the algorithm tracks the whole wave's repetition rate.

Methodology note: we checked this chain against fixed references — 440 Hz reads A4 at 0 cents, 446 Hz reads A4 at +23 cents, silence holds at "--" — and every case passed. The best-known refinement is the YIN algorithm (de Cheveigné & Kawahara, JASA, 2002). Last reviewed: October 2026.

From Voice to Note: The Math, Step by Step

Here is exactly what happens inside the tool when you sing a slightly sharp A at 446 Hz:

  1. Capture: the analyser grabs 2,048 samples — one 43 ms slice of your voice.
  2. Period hunt: autocorrelation finds the strongest repetition at τ ≈ 107.6 samples.
  3. Frequency: f₀ = 48,000 ÷ 107.6 ≈ 446 Hz.
  4. Nearest note: 69 + 12 × log₂(446 ÷ 440) = 69.23, which rounds to MIDI 69 — A4.
  5. Tuning: 1200 × log₂(446 ÷ 440) = +23.5 cents against the 440 Hz target — the display rounds to +23 sharp.
  6. Accuracy score: 100 − 23 = 77%; the needle sits 23/50 of the way toward the meter's right edge.

Every reading on screen is this chain, refreshed many times per second.

Microphone Not Working? Permission and HTTPS Fixes

If START shows the warning box or nothing happens, the cause is almost always a blocked microphone permission. Mic access requires a secure context (HTTPS), per MDN's Web API documentation — this site is served over HTTPS, so the browser should prompt you. If it does not, the permission was likely denied earlier:

  • Chrome / Edge: click the camera icon left of the address bar, set Microphone to Allow, reload.
  • Safari (Mac): Safari menu → Settings → Websites → Microphone → Allow for this site. iPhone: Settings → Safari → Microphone → Allow.
  • Firefox: click the lock icon in the address bar, clear the blocked microphone permission, reload.

Still nothing? Test the mic itself with the microphone test tool — if that page hears you, the fault is this page's permission, not your hardware.

Can a Pitch Detector Hear Chords?

No — this detector hears exactly one pitch at a time. Autocorrelation assumes one repeating period. A chord stacks two or more competing periods, so the strongest match jumps between them and the display flickers between notes. The same happens with piano notes under the sustain pedal, drums, and heavily distorted guitar.

Works well: voice, whistling, flute, trumpet, violin, and single guitar or bass strings played cleanly. For naming chords, use the chord identifier instead — it is built for harmony; this tool is built for single melodic lines.

The 439 Hz Story: Why Concert Pitch Is A440

A440 is a compromise — and the original agreement was 439 Hz, not 440. In 1939 an international conference settled on 440 Hz for the A above middle C, splitting the difference between the lower French pitch (435 Hz, the diapason normal set by law in 1859) and higher pitches used elsewhere. The conference first agreed on 439 Hz; accounts say it moved to 440 partly because 439, a prime number, was awkward to generate with the lab equipment of the day. ISO adopted 440 Hz in 1955 as ISO 16 and reaffirmed it in 1975.

That is this detector's reference. In practice pitch still drifts — many European orchestras tune to 441–444 Hz — so if your ensemble tunes sharp of 440, expect a reading a few cents flat. The tool is right; your reference just differs.

Limitations

  • One note at a time. Chords, double-stops, and percussion produce flickering or wrong readings.
  • Fixed A440 reference. It cannot follow an ensemble tuned to 442 Hz — interpret cents against your own reference.
  • Vibrato, bends, and scoops register as tuning errors. The detector measures what it hears, not what you meant.
  • Range is 50–2000 Hz. Sub-bass below roughly G1 and whistle tones above roughly B6 fall outside detection.
  • Sources under the 0.01 RMS noise gate show "--" instead of a note.
  • Mic processing varies. Phones and laptops apply noise suppression and echo cancellation you cannot always switch off, which can smear the waveform — and room echo adds delayed copies of your note.
  • About 30–60 ms of latency. Fine for practice, not for sample-accurate recording work.
