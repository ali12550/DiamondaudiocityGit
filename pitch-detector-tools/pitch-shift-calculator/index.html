Pitch Shift Calculator: Semitones, Cents & Ratio

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A pitch shift calculator converts a pitch change — measured in semitones and cents — into its exact frequency ratio, varispeed tempo change, and duration factor. Enter +3 semitones and the math says every frequency multiplies by 1.18921: a 120 BPM loop repitched tape-style becomes 142.70 BPM. Key Takeaways

  • A pitch shift of S semitones and C cents multiplies every frequency by r = 2^((S + C/100) / 12) — one semitone is a ratio of about 1.05946.
  • Varispeed (tape/turntable style) changes pitch and tempo together; time-independent DSP shifting changes pitch while the tempo stays put.
  • Shifting vocals without formant preservation creates the "chipmunk effect" — the voice's identity moves with the pitch instead of staying fixed.
  • A turntable's ±8% pitch fader covers roughly +1.33 / −1.44 semitones — the range is asymmetric because pitch ratios are exponential.
  • Ratios multiply, they don't add: two +6-semitone shifts give a ×2.0 octave, not "+12 semitones worth of percentage."

What Is Pitch Shifting?

Definition: Pitch shifting is the process of raising or lowering the pitch of an audio signal by a measured musical interval, stated in semitones and cents. A pitch shift calculator converts that interval into three exact numbers: the frequency ratio applied to every component of the sound, the varispeed tempo change, and the duration factor.

Human pitch perception is logarithmic, not linear. A pitch interval is a ratio, not a fixed number of Hertz: moving from 220 Hz to 440 Hz sounds like the same musical distance as moving from 440 Hz to 880 Hz, because both are a 2:1 ratio — one octave. Western music divides each octave into 12 equal semitones, so one semitone is the twelfth root of 2, a ratio of about 1.0594631.

Pitch shifting is different from transposing MIDI notes (which moves note data, not recorded audio) and from pitch correction (which nudges off-key notes toward the nearest target). Shifting moves the entire signal — fundamental and harmonics together — by one ratio. That is why the calculator's single most important output is the ratio itself.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator takes two inputs — an integer semitone shift and an optional cents fine-tune — and computes the four numbers producers actually need. First it forms the total shift in semitones (total = S + C / 100), then the frequency ratio (r = 2^(total / 12)), the varispeed tempo change ((r − 1) × 100%), and the duration factor (1 / r). Positive values shift up; negative values shift down.

Methodology: the math follows 12-tone equal temperament, the tuning system used by virtually all Western recorded music. The result tiles show total semitones (to two decimals), the frequency ratio (to five), the varispeed speed percentage, and the duration multiplier. We checked this calculator's output against hand-computed values for 12 test shifts from −12 to +12 semitones — every ratio, speed percentage, and duration factor matched. The Pitch Shift Formula and Worked Examples

One formula drives the whole calculator:

r = 2^((S + C / 100) / 12)   ·   f_out = f_in × r

Where S is the semitone shift, C is the cents offset, r is the frequency ratio, and f_in / f_out are the original and shifted frequencies. The handy reference: +12 semitones doubles every frequency (one octave up), −12 halves them (one octave down).

Example 1 — fitting a vocal sample to your beat's key. You drop a female vocal hook in A minor into a track in C minor, so you need +3 semitones. Ratio: r = 2^(3/12) = 1.18921. In varispeed (Re-Pitch) mode the tempo rises by (1.18921 − 1) × 100 = +18.92%, so a 120 BPM vocal becomes 142.70 BPM. If you want to keep the project's 120 BPM, switch to a time-independent mode (Ableton's Complex Pro) and the vocal stays at 120 while the pitch still moves up 3 semitones.

Example 2 — synth unison detune and beat rate. Oscillator 2 is detuned +18 cents above oscillator 1 at 220 Hz. Ratio: r = 2^(18/1200) = 1.01045, so oscillator 2 plays at 220 × 1.01045 = 222.30 Hz. The two oscillators beat against each other at |222.30 − 220| = 2.30 pulses per second — a rich, slow chorus wobble with no dissonance.

Example 3 — DJ turntable math. A Technics-style pitch fader moves ±8% of playback speed. +8% speed gives a ratio of 1.08, which is 12 × log₂(1.08) = +1.33 semitones (+133.24 cents). But −8% gives 12 × log₂(0.92) = −1.44 semitones (−144.35 cents). The range is asymmetric — equal speed percentages do not produce equal semitone steps, because the underlying ratio is exponential.

Reading Your Result

The four tiles answer four production questions. Total semitones is the number to type into a DAW transpose field. Frequency ratio is the mathematical core — multiply any frequency by it to get the shifted value. Varispeed speed shows the tape/vinyl result (+5.95% for one semitone up), and duration factor shows the length change (0.5 means the clip plays in half the time).

Why Ratios Multiply and Percentages Don't Add

A common mistake is adding pitch-shift percentages together. Shifting up 6 semitones is a ×1.41421 ratio (+41.42% speed). Do it twice and you don't get "+82.84%" — you get 1.41421 × 1.41421 = 2.0, exactly one octave, which is +100% speed. The percentages multiply through the ratios; they never add.

The same trap catches detuning: two +50-cent shifts don't add their percentages — each is a ×1.02930 ratio, and the pair multiplies to ×1.05946, exactly one semitone. Whenever you stack shifts, multiply the ratios, not the percentages. The calculator's frequency-ratio tile is the number to multiply; the percentage tile is only valid for a single shift.

Which Pitch-Shift Algorithm to Use in Your DAW

Every major DAW ships several pitch-shift engines, and picking the wrong one is the top cause of bad-sounding shifts. The rule of thumb: match the algorithm to the material — monophonic for single voices, polyphonic for chords and mixes, transient-preserving modes for drums, and varispeed for tape-style effects. Under the hood, nearly all of these engines license zplane élastique (used by Ableton, Cubase, Studio One, FL Studio, Reaper, Pro Tools, and Bitwig), with each DAW wrapping it in its own mode names.

Formants and the Chipmunk Effect

When a person sings different notes, the vocal tract — throat, mouth, nasal cavity — stays the same physical size. Its resonant frequency bands, called formants (F1–F4), stay fixed while the vocal cords change pitch. That fixed formant pattern is a big part of why a voice sounds like that person.

Naive pitch shifting moves everything together: pitch and formants. Shift a vocal up several semitones and the formants land where a much smaller vocal tract would put them — the result is the classic "chipmunk effect," a tiny childlike voice. Shift down and you get the opposite: a cavernous, sluggish "monster" voice. Formant-preserving algorithms split the signal into the pitch excitation and the spectral envelope, move only the pitch, and leave the formants anchored — so a shifted vocal still sounds like the same singer.

Creative producers also shift formants deliberately. Pitch a vocal up while pushing formants down (Ableton's Complex Pro Formants knob, or dedicated tools like SoundToys Little AlterBoy) and you get an androgynous, otherworldly harmony that still sits naturally in a mix — a staple of modern electronic and pop vocal production. So the calculator's ratio tells you how far the notes move; the formant setting decides whether the voice moves with them.

Limitations

  • The math is exact; the sound is not. The ratio, tempo, and duration numbers are precise, but real DSP algorithms add artifacts — phase smearing, transient softening, and metallic ringing — that no calculator can predict.
  • Formants are not part of the ratio. The calculator gives the pitch ratio only. Whether the result sounds like the same singer or a chipmunk depends on your DAW's formant handling, which the math cannot see.
  • Polyphonic material degrades faster. A single flute survives ±7 semitones far better than a full mix; dense, harmonically rich audio accumulates artifacts quickly.
  • Extreme varispeed breaks timing feel. Repitching a drum loop up 12 semitones doubles its tempo — technically correct, but the groove rarely survives the translation.
  • 12-tone equal temperament only. Microtonal music, just intonation, and non-Western tuning systems use different interval math than the 2^(n/12) this page assumes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Pitch shifting is exponential. Shifting by S semitones and C cents multiplies every frequency in the sound by the ratio r = 2^((S + C/100) / 12). A shift of +7 semitones (a perfect fifth) gives r = 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.49831, so a 220 Hz tone becomes 329.63 Hz — every partial scales by the same factor.

Varispeed (tape/turntable style) changes pitch and playback speed together: pitching up raises the tempo and shortens the audio. Time-independent pitch shifting uses DSP such as phase vocoders or zplane élastique to change pitch while keeping the original tempo and duration, at the cost of possible artifacts.

The chipmunk effect happens when pitch is shifted up without formant preservation. Formants are the fixed resonances of the vocal tract that give a voice its identity. Shifting them upward with the pitch makes an adult voice sound like a small child. Formant-preserving algorithms hold the formants in place while the pitch moves.

New BPM = original BPM × r, where r = 2^(semitones / 12). The speed change is (r − 1) × 100%. Shifting a 120 BPM loop up 2 semitones gives r ≈ 1.12246, so the new tempo is 134.70 BPM — a +12.25% speed increase.

Exactly 100 cents make one equal-tempered semitone, and 1200 cents make one octave. Cents exist for micro-tuning: a +15-cent detune adds subtle chorus-like movement to a synth, while a −31.77-cent offset is the exact conversion from A440 to A432.
