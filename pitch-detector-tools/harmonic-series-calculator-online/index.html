Harmonic Series Calculator: Overtones & Partials

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A harmonic series calculator takes any fundamental pitch and lists its harmonics — the integer-multiple overtones (2f, 3f, 4f…) that shape every instrument's timbre — with exact frequencies, note names, and each partial's distance from 12-tone equal temperament in cents. Below, enter a note or frequency to generate up to 256 partials, play each one, or flip to the inverted subharmonic series (utonality).

Key Takeaways

  • The harmonic series is f, 2f, 3f, 4f… — every member is an integer multiple of the fundamental pitch.
  • Watch the terminology: the fundamental is the 1st harmonic, but the 1st overtone is the 2nd harmonic (2f) — the most common beginner mistake.
  • Natural harmonics don't match the piano: the 7th harmonic sits −31.17 cents flat of the equal-tempered minor seventh; the 11th is −48.68 cents flat of the tritone.
  • Real strings stretch sharp of theory (inharmonicity), which is why piano tuners stretch-tune octaves — and why this calculator has an exponent knob.
  • The missing fundamental lets your brain hear bass notes that aren't physically present — it's why voices sound normal on a telephone that cuts everything below 300 Hz.

What Is the Harmonic Series?

Definition: The harmonic series is the set of frequencies a vibrating system produces above its lowest pitch: f, 2f, 3f, 4f, 5f, continuing in whole-number multiples. The lowest member (f) is the fundamental — the pitch you hear as "the note." The members above it are harmonics (also called overtones or partials), and their relative strengths create an instrument's timbre.

The physics is straightforward. A guitar string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in patterns that fit neatly between its fixed points — the whole string, halves, thirds, quarters. Each pattern (a normal mode) vibrates at an integer multiple of the fundamental, with wavelength λn = 2L ÷ n for a string of length L.

Terminology trips up beginners, so lock this in:

TermMeaningExample (f = 100 Hz)
Fundamental (1st harmonic)The lowest member; the perceived pitch100 Hz
HarmonicThe n-th member of the series3rd harmonic = 300 Hz
OvertoneAny member above the fundamental1st overtone = 200 Hz (the 2nd harmonic)
PartialAny component of a complex tonePiano partials are slightly inharmonic — not exact integers

"Partial" is the umbrella term: harmonic partials hit exact integer ratios, while inharmonic partials (bells, stiff piano strings) miss them — and "overtones" never includes the fundamental itself.

How This Calculator Works

Enter a fundamental note or frequency and the tool builds the series with one generalized formula:

fn = f0 × [s(n−1) + 1]e ± α

With the defaults (scaling s = 1.0, exponent e = 1.0, addition α = 0) this collapses to the pure series fn = n × f0. Each row reports the frequency (2 decimals), the rounded MIDI note number, the nearest note name at A4 = 440 Hz, and the cents adjustment — how far the partial sits from the nearest equal-tempered note (positive = sharp, negative = flat). The play button synthesizes each partial as a pure sine tone.

The three extra knobs go further. Scaling (s) re-spaces the partials: s = 0.5 inserts half-harmonics at 1.5f, 2.5f…. Exponent (e) above 1.0 sharpens high partials progressively, modeling string stiffness. The quantize control snaps results to semitone, quarter-tone, or eighth-tone grids, and the subharmonics checkbox flips the series into utonality. The Formulas and Worked Examples

Two formulas cover the pure series — multiply going up, divide going down:

Harmonics (Otonality)
fn = n × f0

Exact integer multiples of the fundamental.

Subharmonics (Utonality)
fn = f0 ÷ n

Undertones descending below the fundamental.

Worked example — harmonics of A2 (110 Hz):

  1. 1st harmonic (fundamental): 1 × 110 = 110.00 Hz (A2)
  2. 2nd: 2 × 110 = 220.00 Hz (A3, one octave up)
  3. 3rd: 3 × 110 = 330.00 Hz — E4 is 329.63 Hz, so this partial sits +1.94 cents sharp
  4. 4th: 4 × 110 = 440.00 Hz (A4, concert pitch)
  5. 5th: 5 × 110 = 550.00 Hz — C♯5 is 554.37 Hz, so this partial sits −13.70 cents flat

Worked example — subharmonics of C2 (65.41 Hz): 65.41 ÷ 2 = 32.70 Hz (C1), ÷ 3 = 21.80 Hz, ÷ 4 = 16.35 Hz. These undertones descend below the fundamental — tick the subharmonics box and compare. Don't know your fundamental in Hz? Look it up with the note-to-frequency converter first.

Why a Clarinet Sounds Nothing Like a Flute

Here is the harmonic series deciding what an instrument is. A clarinet is a cylindrical pipe closed at one end — the reed seals it — and closed pipes support only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5…), because air pressure must peak at the reed end. A flute is open at both ends, and saxophones and oboes are conical, so they support all harmonics.

Two consequences follow. First, the clarinet's register key doesn't jump an octave like a flute's — it jumps a twelfth, straight to the 3rd harmonic, skipping the physically missing 2nd entirely. Second, the clarinet's low register sounds hollow and woody for the same reason a square wave does: both are built from odd harmonics falling off as 1/n, a result Joseph Fourier proved for periodic waveforms in 1822. (Woodwind acoustics: Boston University physics lecture notes.)

Subharmonics and Partch's Utonality

Tick the subharmonics box and the series inverts: f0 ÷ 1, f0 ÷ 2, f0 ÷ 3… These undertones descend below the fundamental as harmonics climb above it. Theorists call the upward series otonality and the inverted one utonality — terms Harry Partch used for the six-note chords of his tuning system.

Partch built his mature music on an 11-limit tonality diamond — ratios built from the primes 1 through 11 — expanding it into a 43-tone just-intonation scale described in Genesis of a Music (1947), and he constructed his own instruments to play it. The idea lives on in studios: subharmonic synthesizers generate bass an octave below the input signal, and EDM producers build sub-bass from the lowest subharmonics of their kicks. Modeling Real Strings With the Exponent Knob

No real string vibrates at exact integer multiples. Steel resists bending, so each partial rings slightly sharp of theory — a phenomenon called inharmonicity, quantified by Harvey Fletcher's 1964 string research: fn = n × f0 × √(1 + Bn²), where B is the inharmonicity coefficient (roughly 0.0001–0.001 for piano strings; short upright strings run higher than long grand strings).

This calculator's exponent knob is a hands-on model of that stiffness. Set e = 1.009 and the 16th partial lands about +43 cents sharp — matching Fletcher's formula with B ≈ 0.0002 at that same partial (we computed both side by side). Piano tuners compensate with stretch tuning: octaves are tuned slightly wide so fundamentals line up with the stretched partials of lower strings, eliminating beats.

One honest caveat: a single exponent is a rough stand-in, not a calibrated B coefficient — it can't track Fletcher's curve at every partial. Use the knob to feel how stiffness reshapes a spectrum, not to tune a piano.

Where Musicians Meet These Numbers Every Day

Pipe organ stops are harmonic-series labels. An 8′ stop sounds the fundamental; 4′ sounds the 2nd harmonic (the octave); the 2⅔′ Nazard sounds the 3rd harmonic (a twelfth above); the 1⅗′ Tierce sounds the 5th harmonic (a seventeenth). Compound stops like the Sesquialtera (2⅔′ + 1⅗′) simply switch on selected harmonics of the 8′ series. (American Guild of Organists; organstops.org.)

Opera singers engineer their harmonics. The "singer's formant" — a cluster of vocal-tract resonances (formants 3–5) near 3 kHz, first analyzed by Johan Sundberg in 1974 — amplifies whichever harmonics fall in that band, letting an unamplified voice cut through an orchestra.

The missing fundamental is everywhere. Traditional telephones pass only 300–3,400 Hz, yet a male voice with a ~150 Hz fundamental sounds normal on a call — your brain rebuilds the pitch from the surviving harmonics (Scientific American; Wikipedia). Alphorn players do the reverse on purpose: their melodies ride the natural 11th harmonic, the "alphorn Fa," about 49 cents flat of the piano's tritone.

Limitations

  • Ideal math, not measured sound. The tool assumes a perfectly flexible string or air column; real instruments add stiffness, end corrections, and bell flare that move partials.
  • The exponent knob is illustrative. It sharpens high partials like real inharmonicity, but one exponent can't reproduce any instrument's actual B-coefficient curve — don't use it to tune a piano.
  • Cents reference 12-TET at A4 = 440 Hz. Historical temperaments, A415 baroque pitch, and A432 aren't modeled.
  • Play buttons make sine tones, not instruments. Real timbre needs each partial's amplitude envelope and phase — this tool shows frequencies only.
  • Subharmonics below ~20 Hz are felt more than heard; check your speakers before judging the utonal series by ear.

Frequently Asked Questions

The harmonic series is the sequence f, 2f, 3f, 4f… — every integer multiple of a fundamental pitch. A vibrating string or air column produces all these frequencies at once; the lowest (f) is the perceived pitch, and the blend of the rest creates the instrument's timbre.

A harmonic is the n-th member of the series (the fundamental is the 1st harmonic); an overtone is any member above it, so the 1st overtone equals the 2nd harmonic. A partial is any component of a complex tone — harmonic partials hit exact integer ratios, inharmonic ones (like a bell's) don't.

Multiply the fundamental frequency by whole numbers: f_n = n × f_0. For A2 at 110 Hz, the harmonics are 220, 330, 440, 550 Hz, and so on. Find your fundamental's Hz with the note-to-frequency converter — or just enter the note above and let the table multiply.

Subharmonics are the inverted harmonic series: f_0 ÷ 1, f_0 ÷ 2, f_0 ÷ 3… descending below the fundamental. Theorists call the upward series otonality and the inverted one utonality — terms from Harry Partch's just-intonation system. Tick the subharmonics box above; producers use the same idea to synthesize sub-bass below a kick drum.

The natural 7th harmonic is 968.83 cents above the fundamental — 31.17 cents flat of the piano's 1000-cent minor seventh. Brass players call it the "harmonic seventh" and use it in blues. Guitarists use the same physics: the 7th-fret harmonic is the pure 3rd harmonic — check yours with our online guitar tuner.

It's your brain reconstructing a pitch that isn't physically present. If you hear harmonics at 300, 400, and 500 Hz, you perceive a 100 Hz fundamental with no 100 Hz tone present. It's why male voices sound normal on telephones that cut everything below 300 Hz — and why small speakers suggest bass they can't reproduce.

Real strings are stiff, so their partials ring sharper than integer multiples — that's inharmonicity, quantified by Fletcher's coefficient B. If a tuner set every note to exact theory, octaves would beat against the stretched partials below. So tuners stretch octaves slightly wide, following the instrument's inharmonicity (Railsback) curve.
