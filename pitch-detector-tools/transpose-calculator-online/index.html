Transpose Calculator: Shift Notes, Chords & Keys

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A transpose calculator shifts notes, chords, or an entire song up or down by any number of semitones while keeping every interval exactly the same. Enter your notes, set the semitone shift, and get the transposed result instantly — no manual counting on the chromatic scale.

Key Takeaways

  • Transposition moves every note by the same interval, so the melody and harmony sound identical in character, just higher or lower.
  • One semitone = one half step = one fret on guitar. Twelve semitones = one octave.
  • A capo on fret n transposes everything up by n semitones.
  • B♭ trumpet parts sound a major 2nd (2 semitones) below written; E♭ alto sax sounds a major 6th (9 semitones) below.
  • This calculator outputs sharp spellings (F♯, C♯); enharmonic flats like G♭ are the same pitch.

What Is Musical Transposition?

Musical transposition is the process of shifting a melody, chord progression, or complete piece of music up or down in pitch by a fixed interval while preserving all internal interval relationships. Because every note moves by the same distance, the music keeps its original harmonic character and emotional feel — it simply sounds in a higher or lower key, which is why transposition is essential for vocalists, capos, and transposing instruments.

Musicians transpose for practical reasons every day. A singer may need a song two semitones lower to sit in their comfortable range. A guitarist puts a capo on the 2nd fret to play easy open chords in a higher key. An arranger rewrites a concert-pitch piano part for B♭ trumpet, shifting every note up a major 2nd so it sounds correct.

How This Transpose Calculator Works

This calculator treats the twelve chromatic pitches as numbered positions 0–11 (C=0 through B=11) and adds your semitone shift with wraparound arithmetic. Flat spellings are accepted too — typing D♭ or B♭ normalizes them to their sharp equivalents (C♯, A♯) before transposing. After the shift is applied modulo 12, any octave crossings adjust the octave number automatically.

Methodology: new index = (note index + semitones + octave shift × 12) mod 12. Transposition Formula and Worked Example

Transposing is simple addition on the chromatic scale. Count the starting note as position 0, then move up (or down) the requested number of half steps:

Transposed note = start note + semitones (mod 12)

Worked example: transpose the I–IV–V progression in C major (C – F – G) up 4 semitones:

  1. C + 4: C→C♯(1)→D(2)→D♯(3)→E(4)
  2. F + 4: F→F♯(1)→G(2)→G♯(3)→A(4)
  3. G + 4: G→G♯(1)→A(2)→A♯(3)→B(4)
  4. Result: E – A – B, which is the I–IV–V progression in E major. Same shape, new key.

Enharmonic Spelling: F♯ or G♭?

After transposing, you may get a sharp spelling where a flat reads better — or the reverse. F♯ and G♭ are the exact same pitch (enharmonic equivalents); the right spelling is the one that fits your key signature.

  • Sharp keys (G, D, A, E major): prefer sharps — write F♯, C♯, G♯.
  • Flat keys (F, B♭, E♭, A♭ major): prefer flats — write G♭, D♭, B♭.
  • Rule of thumb: each letter name should appear once per scale. If your transposed scale already has a G, the altered note is better spelled as F♯ than G♭.

This calculator always outputs sharp spellings, so in flat keys do a quick find-and-replace: C♯→D♭, F♯→G♭, G♯→A♭, A♯→B♭, D♯→E♭.

Transposing for a Vocalist: A 4-Step Workflow

Moving a song into a singer's comfortable key is the most common real-world transposition. Follow these steps:

  1. Find the original key. Check the key signature or the final chord of the song (e.g. original key: E major).
  2. Find the singer's key. Have them hum the chorus; identify the key where the highest note sits comfortably (e.g. they top out happily in C major).
  3. Count the semitones. E down to C is 4 semitones down (E→E♭/D♯ = 1, D = 2, D♭/C♯ = 3, C = 4), so transpose by −4.
  4. Transpose and sanity-check the range. Run the chord progression through the calculator at −4, then confirm the new highest and lowest melody notes still sit inside the singer's range — transposing the key down also drags the low notes down.

The Nashville Number Shortcut

The Nashville Number System writes chords as scale degrees (1, 4, 5) instead of letter names, which makes a progression key-independent. "1 – 5 – 6 – 4" is C–G–Am–F in C major and G–D–Em–C in G major — no recalculation needed.

Use it with this calculator in two passes: first convert your progression to numbers in the original key, then you never need to transpose again — just read the numbers in any new key. Session players in country and pop studios use this daily because the bandleader can call "take it up a whole step" mid-rehearsal and nobody rewrites a chart. Limitations

  • Flat inputs are normalized to sharps. Typing B♭ or E♭ works — flats are converted to their sharp equivalents (A♯, D♯) before transposing. Output always uses sharp spellings.
  • Chord extensions are read as octaves. Entering G7 is interpreted as the note G in octave 7, not a G dominant-7th chord. Transpose the root (G) and re-add the extension (7) yourself.
  • Output uses sharp spellings only. See the enharmonic section above for converting to flats in flat keys.
  • Key signatures are not rewritten. The calculator moves pitches; you still need to update the key signature on your sheet music.
  • Twelve-tone equal temperament assumed. Historical temperaments and microtonal music divide the octave differently.

Frequently Asked Questions

Musical transposition shifts a melody, chord progression, or entire piece up or down by a fixed interval while preserving all internal interval relationships. The song keeps its harmonic character and feel — it simply sounds in a higher or lower key, which is useful for fitting a singer's range or rewriting parts for transposing instruments.

One semitone equals one half step (one piano key or one guitar fret). A whole step is 2 semitones, and an octave is 12 semitones. Common transpositions: minor 3rd = 3 semitones, major 3rd = 4, perfect 4th = 5, perfect 5th = 7.

Each capo fret raises pitch by one semitone. To find what your shapes will sound like, enter the chord roots with a positive semitone number equal to the fret — e.g. G-shape chords with capo 2 (+2) sound in A. To go the other way, enter the target key's chords with a negative shift to find which shapes to finger.

B♭ trumpet sounds a major 2nd (2 semitones) below written pitch, so write the part 2 semitones higher than concert pitch. Enter the concert-pitch notes with +2 semitones. For E♭ alto sax use +9 semitones, and for F horn use +7 semitones.

Concert pitch is the actual sounding pitch — what a piano plays and what the audience hears. Written pitch is what appears on a transposing instrument's sheet music. For non-transposing instruments (piano, flute, violin) they are identical; for a B♭ trumpet, written C sounds as concert B♭.

Both are the same pitch; choose the spelling that fits your key signature. Use sharps in sharp keys (G, D, A major) and flats in flat keys (F, B♭, E♭ major). A good rule: each letter name should appear exactly once in the scale, so pick the spelling that avoids duplicating a letter.

Identify the original key and the key where the singer is comfortable, count the semitones between them, and transpose the whole progression by that amount. Always check that the new lowest notes are still singable too — lowering the key can push verses uncomfortably low.

The interval structure stays identical, so the harmonic character is preserved. Small shifts (±1–2 semitones) are barely noticeable; larger shifts change brightness and energy because the absolute pitch moves into a different part of the instruments' and voice's range.

The Nashville Number System notates chords as scale degrees (1, 4, 5, 6m) instead of letter names, making any progression instantly playable in every key. It is the standard shorthand in country and pop session work, where bandleaders routinely call key changes mid-rehearsal.

The transposed results display sharp spellings (F♯, C♯) because the transposition engine outputs sharps. These are enharmonically identical to their flat equivalents (G♭, D♭) — the same piano key — so respell them to flats when working in flat key signatures. Flat notes you type in (like B♭ or E♭) are accepted and transposed normally; only the output spelling is sharp-only.
