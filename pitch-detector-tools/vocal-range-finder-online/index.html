Vocal Range Finder: Test Your Range & Voice Type

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A vocal range finder measures the lowest and highest notes you can sing and tells you your voice type — soprano, mezzo-soprano, alto, tenor, baritone, or bass. Allow microphone access, sing your lowest and highest comfortable notes, and get your range, octave span, and classification instantly.

Key Takeaways

  • Vocal range is the span from your lowest to highest singable note, written like E2–E4.
  • The six main voice types are soprano, mezzo-soprano, alto, tenor, baritone, and bass — most untrained adults land in the middle two of their gender.
  • Tessitura (where you sing comfortably) matters more than your absolute extremes when choosing songs.
  • Test warmed up, in a quiet room, without vocal fry or falsetto — extremes don't count as usable range.
  • Range can grow with training, typically by a few semitones on each end — not by octaves overnight.

What Is Vocal Range?

Vocal range is the span of musical pitches a person can sing, from the lowest to the highest note, usually written with scientific pitch notation such as C3–C5. In this system C4 is middle C, and each octave number marks a doubling of frequency — so a C3–C5 range covers two full octaves, or 24 semitones, from the lowest C to the highest.

Range alone doesn't fully define a voice. Two singers can share the same E2–E4 span while one sounds like a lyric baritone and the other like a dramatic bass — timbre (tone color), weight, and tessitura complete the picture. That's why this tool reports your range and maps it to the closest standard voice type.

How This Vocal Range Finder Works

This finder captures your voice through your device microphone using the Web Audio API, then runs an autocorrelation pitch detector on each slice of audio. Autocorrelation finds the fundamental frequency by locating the repeating period in the sound wave — the same proven technique behind most tuner apps.

Methodology: the detector accepts frequencies between 60 Hz and 1500 Hz (roughly B1 to F♯6, covering every standard voice type), snaps each reading to the nearest note, and tracks the extremes across two guided phases — lowest note, then highest note. Your span is converted to an octave count (semitones ÷ 12) and matched against the six standard voice-type ranges. For reliable detection, the tool requests raw microphone input with echo cancellation, noise suppression, and auto gain control disabled.

The Note–Frequency Formula and Worked Example

Every note name maps to an exact frequency through twelve-tone equal temperament, anchored at A4 = 440 Hz (the international tuning standard):

f = 440 × 2((m − 69) / 12)  where m is the MIDI note number (A4 = 69) and f is frequency in Hz.

Worked example: find the frequency of middle C (C4, MIDI 60):

  1. Compute the exponent: (60 − 69) / 12 = −9/12 = −0.75
  2. Raise 2 to that power: 2−0.75 ≈ 0.5946
  3. Multiply by 440: 440 × 0.5946 ≈ 261.63 Hz — the standard pitch of middle C.

Each octave doubles the frequency: C5 is 523.25 Hz, C6 is 1046.50 Hz. To convert any frequency back to a note name, try our frequency to note calculator.

How to Use the Vocal Range Finder

  1. Warm up first. Hum scales gently for 3–5 minutes. A cold voice tests up to a 4th narrower than your real range.
  2. Pick your room. Choose a quiet space; background noise and echo confuse the pitch detector. If your mic sounds off, run our microphone test first.
  3. Allow microphone access when the browser asks, then press Start Test.
  4. Sing your lowest note. Glide down on an "ah" vowel until the tone stays clear — no growling vocal fry. Press Confirm Lowest Note.
  5. Sing your highest note. Glide up in full voice (chest/mix, not falsetto) until the tone stays clean, then confirm.
  6. Read your results: lowest note, highest note, total octave span, and your matched voice type. Retest once more and keep the better of two consistent runs.

Range vs Tessitura vs Voice Type: What's the Difference?

These three terms get mixed up constantly, and the confusion leads singers to misclassify themselves. Here's the clean separation:

  • Range = every note you can physically produce, extremes included. It's a measurement.
  • Tessitura = the sub-range where your voice sounds best and tires least — typically the middle 60–70% of your range. It's where songs should live.
  • Voice type = the classification (soprano, tenor…) decided by tessitura + timbre + weight + passaggio location, with range as just one input.

Practical example: a mezzo-soprano and a soprano might both reach C6, but the mezzo's tessitura centers on F4–A5 while the soprano's sits on A4–C6. Same ceiling, different home. When this tool labels you, treat it as a starting hypothesis — sing in the suggested tessitura for a week and notice where you feel most at home.

Why Your Microphone Test Might Lie: 6 Measurement Rules

Most "wrong" results come from testing technique, not the detector. Follow these rules for an honest measurement:

  1. No vocal fry at the bottom. That creaky growl below your modal voice isn't singable pitch — stop at the lowest clear tone.
  2. No falsetto at the top (for classification). Falsetto can add a 4th or more, but classical ranges count full-voice notes. Note it separately if you like.
  3. Warm up. Cold vocal folds are stiffer; testing cold typically costs you 2–4 semitones on top.
  4. Sing, don't slide past breaks silently. Glide slowly and keep the tone connected — jumping over your passaggio hides your real ceiling.
  5. Kill the noise. Fans, traffic, and room echo create false low readings. Close-mic at 15–30 cm in a quiet room.
  6. Take the consistent result. Test twice; if run one says G2–G4 and run two says A2–A4, your range is the overlap you can reproduce, not the union of bests.

The Passaggio Map: Where Voices Naturally Break

The passaggio (Italian for "passage") is the transition zone where the voice shifts gears between registers — the spot where untrained singers "crack." Cracking there is normal physiology, not a flaw; every voice type has one.

Voice typeTypical passaggio zone
SopranoE♭5 – F5
Mezzo-sopranoD5 – E5
ContraltoC5 – D5
TenorE4 – G4
BaritoneD4 – E4
BassC4 – D4

Why this matters for your result: if your confirmed highest note sits just below your type's passaggio, you probably have more range above it once you learn to navigate the transition — your "ceiling" was technique, not anatomy. Targeted exercises (lip trills, sirens through the break) are exactly what voice teachers use to smooth it.

Can You Really Expand Your Range? A Reality Check

Yes — within limits. Consistent training typically adds a few semitones on each end over months: better breath support and fold closure extend the bottom slightly, while register-blending technique unlocks notes above the old break. What training can't do is change your vocal-fold length, which sets the hard floor of your anatomy.

  • Realistic: a baritone (G2–G4) gaining E2–A4 after a year of lessons.
  • Unrealistic: a baritone becoming a tenor — the tessitura and timbre won't follow the extra notes.
  • Red flags: pain, hoarseness lasting over two weeks, or sudden range loss — stop and see a laryngologist, don't push through.

The fastest "expansion" most beginners experience isn't new anatomy at all — it's discovering notes they already owned but couldn't access without technique, especially above the passaggio.

Limitations

  • Detection window is 60–1500 Hz. Notes below ~B1 or whistle-register notes above ~F♯6 won't register.
  • Room and mic quality matter. Laptop mics in echoey rooms produce noisier readings than a close headset mic in a quiet space.
  • One session is a snapshot. Hydration, time of day, illness, and warm-up state shift results by several semitones.
  • Range ≠ voice type. The classification is a nearest-match estimate; timbre, weight, and tessitura need a human teacher's ear.
  • Not a medical tool. It cannot diagnose nodules, polyps, or other vocal conditions — see the disclaimer below.
