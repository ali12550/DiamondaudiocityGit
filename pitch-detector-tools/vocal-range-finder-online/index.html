Vocal Range Finder: Acoustic Voice Type & Tessitura Analysis

Measure your exact vocal frequency boundaries, total octave span, and register transitions with real-time browser audio pitch tracking. Uncover your precise vocal classification—from Contralto and Soprano to Baritone and Bass.

Every human voice is a distinct physiological acoustic instrument composed of the lungs (power source), the vocal folds (oscillators), and the vocal tract (resonant filter). While many singers estimate their capabilities by guessing songs on the radio, scientific vocal assessment requires identifying your sustained fundamental frequencies ($F_0$) without vocal strain. This tool guides you through finding your lowest stable pitch and highest resonant note, mapping your span across the chromatic piano keyboard to reveal both your usable vocal range and your natural voice classification.

Vocal Fold Physiology & Pitch Generation

Vocal pitch is governed by the myoelastic-aerodynamic theory of voice production. As subglottal air pressure from the lungs passes between the adducted (closed) vocal folds, the Bernoulli effect combined with tissue elasticity causes rapid opening and closing cycles. The fundamental frequency ($F_0$) is determined by three physical parameters of the vocal cords:

1. Effective Length ($L$)

Longer vocal cords produce slower oscillation cycles and lower fundamental pitches (typical of adult male basses with folds $17\text{--}25\text{ mm}$ long vs. females with $12.5\text{--}17.5\text{ mm}$).

2. Longitudinal Tension ($T$)

Contraction of the cricothyroid (CT) muscles tilts the thyroid cartilage forward, stretching and thinning the vocal folds to elevate pitch toward higher registers.

3. Vibrating Mass ($M$)

The thyroarytenoid (TA) muscles control internal thickness. In chest voice, full vocal fold mass vibrates; in head voice and falsetto, only the thin mucosal edge oscillates.

Vocal Range vs. Tessitura vs. Passaggio

One of the most frequent misconceptions in singing pedagogy is equating extreme pitch reach with voice category:

Concept Scientific Definition Clinical/Musical Significance
Physical Vocal Range The absolute total span between the lowest phonated sound and highest audible squeak/whistle. Includes unsustainable extremes (glottal fry, uncoordinated falsetto). Does not dictate what key a song should be sung in.
Vocal Tessitura The core pitch zone (usually 1.2 to 1.8 octaves) where a singer produces optimal acoustic resonance with minimal effort. The most critical metric for selecting repertoire, choir section placement, and long-term vocal longevity without fatigue.
The Passaggio (Bridges) The physiological transition points where muscle dominance shifts between the TA (chest) and CT (head) systems. Consists of the Primo Passaggio (entry into mixed voice) and Secondo Passaggio (entry into head voice). Primary criterion used by vocal coaches to classify voice type.

The Four Acoustic Registers in Human Phonation

Pulse Register / Vocal Fry ($M_0$)

The lowest phonatory register, occurring below approximately $70\text{ Hz}$. Vocal folds are short, thick, and lax, remaining closed for up to $90\%$ of each vibratory cycle. Air bubbles intermittently through the glottis, producing a dry popping or clicking rattle. Popularized in sub-harmonic bass singing and modern speech intonation.

Chest / Modal Voice ($M_1$)

The dominant register for speaking and primary singing. Driven predominantly by the thyroarytenoid muscles, the vocal folds vibrate along their entire depth and thickness. Features strong harmonic overtones, rich low-mid acoustic energy, and sympathetic vibrations felt in the sternum and chest wall.

Head Voice / Falsetto ($M_2$)

Activated as pitch rises above the secondo passaggio through cricothyroid elongation. In head voice, only the thin mucosal edges of the vocal folds contact each other, creating a bright, ringing, flute-like tone. In falsetto, the vocal folds remain slightly separated, allowing a breathy slipstream with lower acoustic closure.

Whistle / Flageolet Register ($M_3$)

The highest acoustic register in human phonation, spanning frequencies above $C_6$ ($1046.5\text{ Hz}$) up to $F_7$ and beyond. The posterior portions of the vocal folds tightly clamp shut (damping), allowing only a tiny anterior pinhole opening to oscillate, producing pure, piercing sine-wave-like tones.

Safe Vocal Conditioning & Range Expansion

Expanding your vocal range requires progressive muscular neuromuscular coordination rather than brute physical force. Pushing chest voice beyond the primo passaggio causes hyperfunctional constriction, laryngeal elevation, and risks vocal fold lesions (nodules, polyps, or hemorrhages).

Evidence-Based Daily Vocal Protocol:
  1. SOVT Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract (5 Mins): Phonate glides through a narrow drinking straw into water or perform gentle lip trills. SOVT creates back-pressure (acoustic impedance matching) that lowers phonation threshold pressure and aligns vocal fold edges without muscular collision.
  2. Sirens & 1.5 Octave Arpeggios (5 Mins): Glide smoothly on an "ooh" or "ng" vowel from your comfortable low chest notes up into head voice, crossing your passaggio without abruptly increasing volume or dropping breath support.
  3. Messa di Voce (Dynamic Control): Sustain a single comfortable note, gradually swelling from pianissimo ($pp$) to forte ($f$) and back to pianissimo ($pp$). This trains subtle cricothyroid and thyroarytenoid coordination.
  4. Hydration & Laryngeal Rest: Vocal fold mucosal tissue requires systemic hydration. Drink ambient-temperature water throughout the day and avoid heavy clearing of the throat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Vocal range is measured as the musical interval between the lowest and highest pitches a vocalist can sustain with controlled pitch and tone. It is commonly expressed in Scientific Pitch Notation (such as E2 to G4) and quantified by total semitones and octaves (e.g., 28 semitones, or 2 octaves and a fourth).

Physical vocal range includes every sound a singer can physically phonate, from glottal vocal fry to extreme whistle tones. In contrast, tessitura refers specifically to the comfortable, resonant pitch span where a singer's voice sounds best and can perform sustained repertoire without vocal fatigue, strain, or loss of timbre.

The primary classical voice types are: Bass (E2–E4), Baritone (A2–A4), Tenor (C3–C5), Contralto (E3–E5), Mezzo-Soprano (A3–A5), and Soprano (C4–C6). Some male vocalists also specialize as Countertenors (E3–E5) using developed falsetto and head voice.

The passaggio is the anatomical transition zone between vocal registers (such as chest voice to mixed voice, and mixed voice to head voice). The exact pitch locations where these breaks occur (the primo and secondo passaggio) are the most reliable physiological indicators of a singer's true voice type, far more so than extreme high or low notes.

An untrained singer typically possesses an active range of 1.5 to 2 octaves (approximately 18 to 24 semitones). With classical or contemporary vocal training, singers routinely develop a usable range of 2.5 to 3 octaves. World-class vocalists with exceptional whistle or sub-harmonic control may span 4 or more octaves.
