The Physics of Musical Pitch: Converting Notes to Fundamental Frequencies (Hz)

In Western music theory, notes are discrete symbolic labels—such as $C_4$, $F\sharp_3$, or $B\flat_5$. In acoustic physics, however, these musical notes correspond to exact continuous vibration rates measured in Hertz (Hz), representing cycles per second.

Modern instruments and digital synthesizers are tuned according to 12-Tone Equal Temperament (12-TET). In this mathematical tuning system, the octave is divided into 12 semitones spaced by a constant logarithmic frequency multiplier equal to the twelfth root of two:

$$\sqrt[12]{2} = 2^{\frac{1}{12}} \approx 1.059463094$$

Every time you ascend one semitone (e.g. from C to C#), frequency is multiplied by $\approx 1.059463$. Ascending 12 semitones multiplies frequency by $(2^{1/12})^{12} = 2.0$, creating an exact octave doubling.

Mathematical Equations & Acoustic Parameters

1. Frequency from MIDI Note

Computes fundamental frequency $f$ from MIDI note number $m$ (where $A_4 = 69$):

$$f = A_4 \times 2^{\frac{m - 69}{12}}$$

At standard $A_4 = 440\text{ Hz}$, Middle C ($m = 60$) yields $440 \times 2^{-9/12} = 261.63\text{ Hz}$.

2. Acoustic Wavelength

Calculates the physical distance spanned by one full acoustic cycle in air at 20°C ($v = 343\text{ m/s}$):

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{343}{f}\text{ meters}$$

Bass notes span meters; high treble notes span mere centimeters.

3. Wave Period

Measures the duration in milliseconds required for one single waveform oscillation:

$$T = \frac{1000}{f}\text{ ms}$$

For $A_4$ ($440\text{ Hz}$), each wave oscillation takes $1000 / 440 = 2.27\text{ ms}$.

Sound Engineering & DAW Production Workflows

1. Sub-Bass & Kick Tuning

In electronic music, setting an 808 sub-bass to the exact frequency of your root note prevents low-end phase cancellations. If your track is in D Minor, tune your sub fundamental to $D_1$ (36.71 Hz) or $D_2$ (73.42 Hz).

2. Resonant Filter Tracking

When calibrating self-oscillating synth filters (keyboard tracking set to 100%), knowing exact note frequencies allows you to play the filter's resonance as a pure sine wave lead instrument.

3. Room Acoustic Modes

Studio room dimensions create standing waves (room modes). An axial mode at 65 Hz corresponds exactly to $C_2$, meaning every time a bassline hits C, that specific note will boom uncomfortably in an untreated control room.

Frequently Asked Questions

The frequency of any note in 12-Tone Equal Temperament is computed using: f = A4 × 2^((m - 69) / 12), where 'm' is the MIDI note number (0 to 127) and A4 is the reference concert pitch (typically 440 Hz). Middle C (C4, MIDI 60) yields f = 440 × 2^(-9/12) = 261.63 Hz.

Wavelength (λ) is the physical distance between consecutive sound wave crests in air, calculated as λ = v / f, where 'v' is the speed of sound (343 m/s at 20°C). Deep bass notes have immense physical wavelengths (e.g. C1 at 32.7 Hz has a 10.5-meter wave), while high treble notes have tiny wavelengths (e.g. C8 at 4186 Hz has an 8.2-centimeter wave).

The standard MIDI protocol represents musical notes as integers from 0 to 127. Under standard Scientific Pitch Notation (SPN), C-1 is MIDI 0, C0 is MIDI 12, Middle C (C4) is MIDI 60, Concert A (A4) is MIDI 69, and G9 is MIDI 127. Each increment of 1 represents exactly one semitone.

While A4 = 440 Hz is the standard international ISO pitch, European orchestras (especially in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland) commonly tune to 442 Hz or 443 Hz for added acoustic brightness and string brilliance. Baroque ensembles often tune down to 415 Hz, and alternative music traditions explore 432 Hz for warm mathematical harmonies.

Producers convert notes to frequencies to tune sub-bass oscillators to the root key of a song, set parametric EQ resonant boosts on kick drum fundamental tones, sync LFO frequency rates to musical pitch, and tune acoustic room bass traps to room resonance modes.

The period (T) is the exact time required for one complete acoustic cycle to occur, calculated as T = 1 / f seconds (or 1000 / f in milliseconds). For A4 (440 Hz), one complete wave cycle completes every 2.27 milliseconds.
