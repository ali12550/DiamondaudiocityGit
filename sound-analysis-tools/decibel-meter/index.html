The Physics and Mathematics of the Decibel Scale

The decibel (dB) is not an absolute unit of physical mass or distance; it is a dimensionless logarithmic ratio between a measured quantity and a standardized acoustic reference level. Because human ears register acoustic pressure across a vast range from $0.00002\,\text{Pa}$ to over $200\,\text{Pa}$ (a factor of $10,000,000:1$), linear scales are impractical.

Sound Pressure Level (SPL) Mathematical Formula:

In airborne acoustics, Sound Pressure Level ($L_p$) is defined by the international ISO standard as:

L_p (dB SPL) = 20 × log₁₀(p_rms / p₀)

Where:

  • p_rms = Root Mean Square sound pressure of the measured wave in Pascals (Pa).
  • p₀ = Standard reference sound pressure in air = 20 micropascals (20 μPa = 2 × 10⁻⁵ N/m²), corresponding to the threshold of hearing for a healthy human ear at 1,000 Hz.

For sound intensity and acoustic power, where energy is proportional to the square of pressure ($I \propto p^2$), the formula employs a factor of 10: $L_I = 10 \times \log_{10}(I / I_0)$.

The 3 dB Energy Rule vs. The 10 dB Psychoacoustic Rule

A crucial source of confusion in audio engineering is the difference between physical acoustic energy and subjective human loudness:

The +3 dB Energy Rule (Physical Power)

Because $\log_{10}(2) \approx 0.3010$, multiplying sound power or amplifier wattage by a factor of 2 yields: $10 \times \log_{10}(2) = +3.01\text{ dB}$. Adding a second identical speaker doubles acoustic power, increasing output by exactly +3 dB.

The +10 dB Psychoacoustic Rule (Perceived Loudness)

Due to the human auditory cortex compressing dynamic range, an increase of +3 dB is barely perceptible as a slight volume bump. A sound must increase by roughly +10 dB (a tenfold increase in acoustic energy, $10 \times p^2$) before a typical human listener judges it to be twice as loud.

Frequency Weighting Networks: dBA vs. dBC vs. dBZ

The human ear does not possess a flat frequency response. According to the ISO 226 equal-loudness contours (Fletcher-Munson curves), human hearing is exceptionally sensitive between 2 kHz and 5 kHz (the resonant frequencies of the human ear canal where speech intelligibility resides) and significantly less sensitive to deep bass below 100 Hz:

Weighting Curve Filter Characteristics Acoustic Rationale Primary Applications
dBA (A-Weighting) Aggressive high-pass roll-off: -50 dB at 20 Hz, -16 dB at 125 Hz, +1.2 dB peak at 2.5 kHz. Replicates the 40-phon equal-loudness contour of quiet-to-moderate human hearing. OSHA workplace noise compliance, environmental noise ordinances, classroom monitoring.
dBC (C-Weighting) Flatter response: -3 dB at 31.5 Hz and 8 kHz; only rolls off subsonic & ultrasonic frequencies. Replicates human auditory response at high sound levels (90–100 phon) where ear sensitivity flattens. Live concert mixing, subwoofer calibration, mechanical HVAC/engine rumble analysis.
dBZ (Z-Weighting) Completely linear and unweighted (Zero weighting) from 10 Hz to 20,000 Hz ($pm 0.5\text{ dB}$). Raw physical sound pressure measurement with zero perceptual compensation. Laboratory acoustic testing, audio equipment specification, scientific noise emission surveys.

Occupational Hearing Safety: OSHA vs. NIOSH Standards

Prolonged exposure to excessive decibel levels causes permanent sensorineural hearing loss through mechanical destruction of stereocilia hair cells inside the cochlea. Occupational safety bodies regulate maximum daily noise doses using exchange rates:

Sound Pressure Level (dBA) OSHA Permissible Limit (5 dB Exchange) NIOSH Recommended Limit (3 dB Exchange) Typical Real-World Equivalent
80 dBA Unlimited (no action) 25 hours Noisy restaurant, garbage disposal, highway traffic.
85 dBA 16 hours 8 hours (Daily Action Level) Heavy city traffic, commercial food blender.
90 dBA 8 hours (OSHA PEL) 2 hours 31 min Lawnmower, passing diesel truck, power workshop.
95 dBA 4 hours 47 minutes Motorcycle at 25 feet, table saw, circular saw.
100 dBA 2 hours 15 minutes Nightclub, subway train arriving, pneumatic drill.
105 dBA 1 hour 4.7 minutes Sporting stadium crowd roar, chainsaw, helicopter.
110 dBA 30 minutes 1.5 minutes Rock concert front row, car horn at 1 meter.
120+ dBA 0 minutes (Immediate danger) 0 minutes (Threshold of pain) Siren at close range, jet takeoff, gunshot, firecracker.

Notice that while OSHA permits 90 dBA for 8 hours using a 5 dB exchange rate, NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) advocates an 85 dBA threshold with a scientifically rigorous 3 dB energy-doubling exchange rate to protect 96% of the workforce from permanent hearing loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

The online decibel meter uses the Web Audio API to stream raw audio from your device microphone. It computes the Root Mean Square (RMS) voltage of discrete audio buffers and calculates dBFS (decibels relative to full scale) using the formula $\text{dBFS} = 20 \times \log_{10}(\text{RMS} / \text{Max})$. Optional FFT spectral filters apply dBA or dBC weighting curves to simulate human acoustic perception.

In air acoustics, 0 dB SPL represents the absolute threshold of human hearing at 1,000 Hz, standardized as a root-mean-square sound pressure of 20 micropascals ($20\,\mu\text{Pa}$, or $2 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{N/m}^2$). Any sound pressure level in dB SPL is computed as $L_p = 20 \times \log_{10}(p / p_0)$.

dBA (A-weighting) mimics the human ear's sensitivity at moderate volumes by rolling off low sub-bass and ultra-high frequencies based on Fletcher-Munson curves; it is mandatory for OSHA workplace safety. dBC (C-weighting) provides a flatter response with modest bass roll-off, ideal for measuring subwoofers and concert peaks. dBZ (Zero-weighting) represents completely unweighted, linear acoustic measurement from 10 Hz to 20 kHz.

The 3 dB rule states that an increase of +3 dB represents an exact doubling of acoustic sound power (energy). The 10 dB rule relates to psychoacoustics: because the human brain perceives loudness logarithmically, an increase of approximately +10 dB is required for an average human listener to perceive the sound as twice as loud.

OSHA establishes a Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL) of 90 dBA for an 8-hour workday with a 5 dB exchange rate (95 dBA is safe for 4 hours, 100 dBA for 2 hours). NIOSH recommends a stricter, safer standard of 85 dBA for 8 hours with a 3 dB exchange rate (88 dBA is safe for 4 hours, 91 dBA for 2 hours, and 100 dBA for only 15 minutes).

Because consumer smartphone and laptop microphones lack factory SPL calibration and apply automatic gain control (AGC), you can calibrate this meter by generating a 1 kHz reference tone at a known SPL level (or placing a certified handheld SPL meter next to your microphone) and adjusting the Calibration Offset slider until both readings match.
