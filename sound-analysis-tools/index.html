What Is Sound Analysis?

Definition: Sound analysis is the measurement of audio signals to understand their level, loudness, clarity, and noise content. Online sound analysis tools perform these measurements in your browser using your device's microphone and the Web Audio API — no installed software needed.

Use them to check whether a room is too loud, verify a microphone works before a call or stream, confirm a podcast's loudness hits platform targets, or simply see what sound looks like as a waveform. They answer "how loud is this, really?" with a number instead of a guess.

dB vs. dBA vs. LUFS: Three Numbers, Three Jobs

People say "decibels" for three different measurements. Mixing them up is the most common loudness mistake.

UnitMeasuresUse it for
dB SPLRaw sound pressure vs. 20 micropascalsPhysical loudness of a space (the Decibel Meter)
dBASound pressure weighted for human hearingHearing safety, noise regulations, workplace limits
LUFSPerceived loudness over time (ITU-R BS.1770)Podcasts, streaming, broadcast delivery (the Loudness Meter)

Everyday reference points: a whisper is about 30 dB, normal conversation about 60 dB, a rock concert about 110 dB. Because the decibel scale is logarithmic, 70 dB isn't "a bit louder" than 60 dB — it carries ten times the sound energy.

How Loud Is Too Loud? OSHA and NIOSH Limits

Hearing damage depends on both level and duration. Two US authorities define the limits — and they disagree on the math, which is worth knowing.

OSHA (legal requirement)NIOSH (health recommendation)
8-hour limit90 dBA (permissible exposure limit)85 dBA (recommended exposure limit)
Action level85 dBA — triggers a hearing conservation program—
Exchange rate5 dB: every +5 dB halves allowed time3 dB: every +3 dB halves allowed time
At 100 dBA2 hours allowed15 minutes allowed

The NIOSH 3 dB exchange rate follows physics (every 3 dB doubles sound energy); OSHA's 5 dB rate is the legally enforceable minimum in US workplaces. For personal listening, the conservative move is the NIOSH math: 85 dBA for 8 hours, 88 for 4, 91 for 2. Sources: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95; NIOSH noise criteria.

Why Browser Meters Aren't Calibrated SPL Meters

An honest limitation worth understanding: a $2,000 Class 1 sound level meter is calibrated against a known reference tone so its dB readings are absolute. A browser page has no idea how sensitive your microphone is — a phone mic, a headset, and a USB condenser all report different raw levels for the same room.

What the tools can do reliably: relative measurement. "The room got 10 dB louder when the AC kicked in" is trustworthy; "the room is exactly 52 dB" is approximate. Two more gotchas: many devices apply automatic gain control (AGC) that fights the meter — disable mic "enhancements" in your OS settings for steadier readings — and browsers can't defeat a mic's own noise floor, typically 30–40 dBA on built-in mics.

Limitations

  • Not calibrated instruments. Readings are approximate in absolute terms; trust them for comparisons, not compliance measurements.
  • OS audio processing interferes. Automatic gain control, noise suppression, and "enhancements" can skew readings — turn them off for measurement.
  • Mic quality bounds everything. Built-in mics have higher noise floors and uneven frequency response versus measurement microphones.
  • No legal standing. These tools can't certify workplace noise compliance — that requires calibrated equipment and a qualified hygienist.
