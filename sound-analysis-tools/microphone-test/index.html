Microphone Test Tool (Online & Free)

Click "Start" to allow access to your microphone and test the input.

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A microphone test checks that your mic works, shows your live input level, and reveals problems like clipping or background noise — before your recording, stream, or call. Press Start Mic Test and speak normally.

Key Takeaways

  • Healthy mic level: peaks between −12 and −6 dBFS, average around −18 dBFS.
  • Red = clipping. Back off the gain or the mic until peaks stay out of the red.
  • Silence should read near zero — a bouncing meter with no speech means background noise.
  • Disable auto gain control (AGC) in OS settings before testing, or readings lie.
  • This test checks function and levels, not frequency response or studio-grade quality.

What Is a Microphone Test?

A microphone test is a quick check that your microphone captures audio correctly: it verifies the browser can access the mic, displays your live input level, and exposes issues like clipping, low gain, or background noise. It takes thirty seconds and prevents the classic disaster of recording an hour of audio with a dead or distorted mic.

This tool uses the getUserMedia API to open your default recording device and draws a real-time level meter. Speak, watch the meter, run the 3-second silence check below — you'll know exactly where your setup stands.

How This Microphone Test Works

Clicking Start Mic Test asks the browser for microphone permission, then connects the live stream to an analyser that measures input amplitude many times per second and drives the on-screen meter. Stop Test releases the microphone so no other app is blocked.

Methodology: the meter maps instantaneous amplitude to a green–yellow–red scale — green for healthy signal, yellow approaching hot, red at digital clipping (0 dBFS). The 3-second silence protocol (below) separates your voice from the room's noise floor. Validation method: clap once loudly — the meter should spike into red momentarily, then fall back; if it never moves, the wrong input device is selected.

Target Levels and Worked Example

Digital audio measures level in dBFS (decibels relative to full scale), where 0 dBFS is the clipping ceiling and everything else is negative. Your targets:

Use casePeak targetAverage target
Music/podcast recording−12 to −6 dBFS≈ −18 dBFS
Live streaming−12 to −6 dBFS−18 to −12 dBFS
Video calls−20 to −12 dBFS≈ −20 dBFS

Worked example: your meter peaks at −24 dBFS while speaking normally — 12 dB too quiet for recording. Since +6 dB ≈ double the digital amplitude, you need roughly +12 dB of gain (about 4× amplitude). Raise the OS input level or interface gain until peaks kiss −12 to −6 dBFS, leaving headroom for laughs and emphasis.

How to Test Your Microphone

  1. Click Start Mic Test and choose Allow when the browser asks for microphone access.
  2. Speak normally — count "one, two, three" at your real recording volume and distance.
  3. Watch the meter: healthy speech lives in the green, occasionally touching yellow (−12 to −6 dBFS).
  4. Run the 3-second silence test: stop talking and stay quiet — the meter should fall near zero.
  5. Adjust gain in your OS sound settings or interface until both checks pass.
  6. Click Stop Test to release the microphone when done.

Microphone Not Working? Troubleshooting Checklist

Work through these in order — they solve nearly every "mic not working" case:

  1. Permission: click the lock/camera icon in the address bar and set microphone to Allow, then reload.
  2. Default device: in OS sound settings, confirm your intended mic is the default recording device (not "default communications device" only).
  3. Browser device picker: some browsers let sites pick a device — check the site's mic selector if one appears.
  4. App conflicts: close Zoom/Teams/Discord — another app may be holding the mic exclusively.
  5. Hardware: unplug and replug USB mics; for XLR mics confirm +48V phantom power is on (condensers need it; USB mics never do).
  6. Privacy switches: many laptops have a physical mic mute key or shutter — check it isn't engaged.

AGC: The Setting That Silently Ruins Your Levels

Automatic gain control (AGC) is an OS feature that rides your mic gain up and down to "normalize" speech. It makes testing meaningless — the meter dances on its own — and it pumps background noise up during pauses. Turn it off before any serious test or recording: Windows → Sound settings → Input → disable "Automatically adjust microphone settings"/"audio enhancements"; macOS → no system AGC, but check individual apps (Zoom's "automatically adjust volume" is a common culprit); Chrome → chrome://settings/content/microphone has no AGC toggle, so fix it at the OS level.

Limitations

  • Checks function, not fidelity. The test confirms signal path and levels — it cannot measure frequency response, self-noise specs, or "studio quality."
  • Browser processing applies. Some browsers apply noise suppression to getUserMedia streams; for a raw check, prefer Chrome/Edge with OS enhancements off.
  • One device at a time. The test uses your default input; to check a second mic, switch the OS default and re-run.
  • No recording. Nothing is captured or stored — if you need a sample, record a voice memo separately after the level check.
