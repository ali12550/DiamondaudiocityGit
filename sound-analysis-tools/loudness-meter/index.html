How to Use the Online Loudness Meter

  1. Connect Your Audio Feed: Click Start Loudness Meter and allow microphone or loopback audio input. Play your mix, vocal performance, or audio master in real-time.
  2. Monitor Momentary & Short-Term Readings: Observe Momentary LUFS (M) to track momentary dynamic spikes, and watch Short-Term LUFS (S) over 3-second blocks to verify that verses, bridges, and drops hit intended dynamic targets.
  3. Check Full-Track Integrated LUFS: Let your entire song play through. The Integrated LUFS (I) meter calculates overall weighted loudness, indicating whether your track aligns with Spotify (-14 LUFS), Apple Music (-16 LUFS), or broadcast standards (-23 LUFS).
  4. Audit True Peak Headroom: Verify that True Peak (dBTP) remains comfortably below -1.0 dBTP to prevent digital-to-analog converter distortion and inter-sample clipping on lossy MP3/AAC encoders.

The Science of Loudness: ITU-R BS.1770 & EBU R128

During the notorious "Loudness Wars" of the 1990s and 2000s, mastering engineers competed to produce the loudest CDs by squashing dynamic range with brickwall limiters. Because conventional digital peak meters (measuring dBFS) only register maximum instantaneous sample voltages, severely distorted tracks appeared normal while destroying acoustic punch and listener ear fatigue.

To solve this industry-wide crisis, the International Telecommunication Union established ITU-R BS.1770, creating the LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale) standard (also known as LKFS in North America). Unlike simple electrical peak or RMS meters, LUFS models human ear physiology using a specialized K-weighting curve:

The Two-Stage K-Weighting Psychoacoustic Filter:
  1. Stage 1 (Head Acoustic Simulation): A high-frequency shelving filter boosting frequencies above $1.5\text{ kHz}$ by approximately $+4\text{ dB}$, simulating acoustic refraction and amplification around the human head and ear canal.
  2. Stage 2 (High-Pass Pre-Filter): A second-order high-pass filter with a cutoff near $38\text{ Hz}$ that rolls off deep sub-bass frequencies, reflecting the human ear's natural low-frequency insensitivity at typical listening levels.

After filtering, channel energies are summed with directional weighting and converted to LUFS: $\text{LUFS} = -0.691 + 10 \times \log_{10}\left(\sum z_i\right)$.

The Four Core Loudness Metrics Explained

Momentary Loudness ($M$)

Calculated over an un-gated sliding 400-millisecond rectangular time window. Momentary loudness reveals immediate transient bursts—such as heavy snare hits, aggressive brass stabs, or 808 bass drops.

Short-Term Loudness ($S$)

Calculated over a continuous 3-second sliding time window. Short-term loudness is the primary metric used during mixing and live broadcasts to maintain consistent perceived volume between dialogue, verses, and choruses.

Integrated Loudness ($I$)

The single overall target value representing the entire duration of a track or television program. It uses dual gating: an absolute gate at -70 LUFS (ignoring silence) and a relative gate at -10 LU below un-gated loudness (preventing quiet intros or pauses from skewing the reading).

True Peak ($dBTP$)

Employs minimum 4x oversampling to reconstruct the continuous analog waveform. This detects inter-sample peaks (ISPs)—invisible voltage spikes that peak above 0 dBFS between consecutive digital samples and distort consumer DACs.

The "Loudness Penalty" and How to Avoid It

Many self-mastering artists mistakenly crush their tracks to -8 or -6 LUFS believing louder files stand out. In modern streaming environments, the opposite happens: The Loudness Penalty.

When you submit a track mastered at -8 LUFS to Spotify or YouTube (-14 LUFS target), their servers automatically attenuate your audio by -6 dB. Because the track was heavily limited, its punchy kick transients and snare snaps were permanently flattened. When placed side-by-side with a dynamic mix mastered to -14 LUFS, the dynamic track will sound punchier, wider, and clearer at the exact same perceived volume!

Frequently Asked Questions

Peak decibels (dBFS) measure the highest instantaneous electrical or digital voltage level, while RMS measures average electrical energy across a window. Neither corresponds to human hearing. LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale), equivalent to LKFS, applies a psychoacoustic K-weighting curve that boosts upper-mid frequencies and attenuates bass, matching the human ear's non-linear perception of loudness as defined in ITU-R BS.1770.

Momentary LUFS (M) measures loudness over an un-gated sliding 400-millisecond window for tracking instantaneous transients. Short-Term LUFS (S) measures loudness over a 3-second sliding window for monitoring vocal and verse/chorus flow. Integrated LUFS (I) calculates the overall program loudness of the entire song from start to finish, applying an absolute gate at -70 LUFS and a relative gate at -10 LU to ignore pauses.

True Peak (dBTP) measures the maximum analog voltage that will occur when digital samples are converted to sound by a DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter). Even if digital sample peaks remain below 0.0 dBFS, inter-sample peaks between adjacent samples can exceed 0 dBFS and cause harsh analog clipping distortion. Major streaming platforms require True Peak levels not to exceed -1.0 dBTP (or -2.0 dBTP for hot masters).

Standard target Integrated LUFS levels are: Spotify (-14 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP), Apple Music (-16 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP), YouTube (-14 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP), Tidal (-14 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP), Amazon Music (-14 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP), and Broadcast Television EBU R128 (-23.0 LUFS, -1.0 dBTP) or ATSC A/85 (-24.0 LUFS, -2.0 dBTP).

If your song is mastered to -8 LUFS, Spotify's loudness normalization algorithm simply attenuates your overall volume down by 6 dB to match -14 LUFS. You gain zero volume advantage over other tracks, but your song suffers from crushed transients and loss of dynamic punch compared to tracks that retained breathing room.

Loudness Range (LRA), measured in Loudness Units (LU), quantifies the statistical dynamic variation between the quietest and loudest parts of a track (excluding silence and extreme transients). A tight pop master typically exhibits an LRA of 3 to 6 LU, while an expressive classical symphony or cinematic film score might span 15 to 25 LU.
